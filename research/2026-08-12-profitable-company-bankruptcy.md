# Franklin Research Dossier — A Profitable Company Can Still Go Bankrupt

## Executive Summary

A profitable company can fail because profit and liquidity answer different questions. Profit records income and expenses over a period under accrual accounting; liquidity asks whether cash and available funding can cover obligations on the dates they fall due. A credit sale can create revenue, profit, and a receivable before the customer pays. Growth can therefore increase reported profit while absorbing cash into receivables and inventory. Capital expenditure, debt principal, taxes, dividends, and a refinancing deadline can widen the gap. The timing mismatch is normally bridged with cash reserves, supplier credit, a revolving facility, or fresh capital. Failure becomes possible when the gap exceeds those resources. The video should teach that mechanism with a simple numerical company, then test it against Carillion—carefully framed as a case of weak cash conversion and unreliable reported profits, rather than a pure example of a genuinely healthy profitable company running out of cash.

## Recommended Editorial Angle

**The viewer will leave believing that a company can earn profit and still miss payroll because the income statement records economic activity while the payment calendar demands cash. Growth can make that deadline arrive faster.**

The counterintuitive part is not merely that “profit is not cash.” That phrase is familiar and inert. The useful causal model is:

1. The company commits cash to labour, suppliers, inventory, equipment, or debt service.
2. Accounting can recognise revenue and profit before the related customer cash arrives.
3. Faster growth can enlarge the amount trapped between payment and collection.
4. Cash reserves and financing normally bridge the timing difference.
5. A covenant breach, lender refusal, maturity wall, customer delay, or loss of confidence removes the bridge.
6. A payment falls due. The company cannot make it. The legal and operational crisis begins on that date, regardless of the profit previously reported for the quarter or year.

The best opening sentence is the contradiction in numerical form:

> On paper, the company has made €150,000. In its bank account, it is €50,000 short. Both numbers are correct.

The open loop is that **more sales caused the shortfall to arrive sooner**.

## Definitions That Must Stay Separate

| Term | Plain-English meaning | What it answers | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Profit / net income** | Income less expenses recognised for a period | Did recognised economic activity create accounting earnings? | Recognition and cash movement can occur at different times. Estimates and judgements affect some items. |
| **Operating cash flow (CFO)** | Cash generated or consumed by the main business, including working-capital movements | Did operations produce cash during the period? | Can be temporarily improved by delaying suppliers or using arrangements whose classification requires scrutiny. |
| **Free cash flow (FCF)** | Commonly CFO less capital expenditure | How much internally generated cash remains after maintaining or expanding the asset base? | It is a non-GAAP/non-IFRS measure and definitions vary. State the formula used. |
| **Liquidity** | Cash and reliably available funding for near-term needs | Can the company meet upcoming cash requirements? | An unused facility is useful only if it remains available and its conditions can be met. |
| **Solvency** | Ability to meet obligations, assessed through cash-flow and/or balance-sheet tests depending on jurisdiction | Can the company meet debts as they fall due, and are its assets sufficient for its liabilities? | The precise legal test is jurisdiction-specific. A temporary cash squeeze with credible bridge finance is not automatically legal insolvency. |
| **Bankruptcy / insolvency proceeding** | A formal legal process for restructuring or winding down | What legal framework applies after distress or a filing? | “Bankruptcy” is not a universal synonym for insolvency. UK companies generally enter administration or liquidation; US Chapter 11 is often a reorganisation process. |
| **EBITDA** | Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation | A rough view of operating earnings before selected costs | It ignores working capital, capital expenditure, debt principal, interest, and taxes. It is not a bank balance. |

Official grounding:

- IAS 1 requires financial statements other than cash-flow information to use the accrual basis. It also says going-concern assessment may require considering profitability, debt repayment schedules, and replacement financing together. [IAS 1, paragraphs 25–28](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/html-standards/english/2024/issued/ias1.html)
- IAS 7 separates operating, investing, and financing cash flows. Under the indirect method, profit is adjusted for non-cash transactions, accruals and deferrals, and investing/financing items. [IFRS Foundation summary of IAS 7](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-7-statement-of-cash-flows/)
- The SEC’s financial-reporting guidance defines liquidity around the ability to generate adequate cash for cash needs and emphasises material cash requirements, sources, uses, trends, and uncertainties. [SEC Financial Reporting Manual, Topic 9](https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-corporation-finance/financial-reporting-manual/frm-topic-9)

## The Core Accounting Mechanism

### 1. A sale can create profit before it creates cash

Suppose a company delivers a product today and gives the customer 60 days to pay. Once the applicable recognition conditions are satisfied, the company can record:

- revenue on the income statement;
- a receivable on the balance sheet;
- the related cost of goods sold as an expense;
- and accounting profit equal to the difference.

Its bank balance does not rise until the customer pays. The receivable is an asset because it represents an enforceable claim to future cash, but it cannot necessarily be used to pay wages this Friday. The company may be able to borrow against it or sell it, but that is a financing step, with cost and conditions.

### 2. Working capital is where growth consumes cash

A simplified operating working-capital investment is:

\[
\text{Operating working capital} \approx \text{Receivables} + \text{Inventory} - \text{Payables}
\]

If receivables and inventory rise faster than payables, more cash becomes tied up in the operating cycle. The indirect cash-flow bridge can be expressed as:

\[
\text{CFO} \approx \text{Net income} + \text{Non-cash charges} - \Delta AR - \Delta Inventory + \Delta AP \pm \text{other operating adjustments}
\]

Where:

- \(AR\) is accounts receivable;
- \(AP\) is accounts payable;
- an increase in receivables consumes cash relative to profit;
- an increase in inventory consumes cash;
- an increase in payables temporarily preserves cash because suppliers have not yet been paid.

The cash conversion cycle is a second useful lens:

\[
\text{CCC} = \text{Days inventory outstanding} + \text{Days sales outstanding} - \text{Days payables outstanding}
\]

A longer cycle means the company finances more days between paying for inputs and collecting from customers. This is an operating-finance need, not proof of a bad business. The danger comes when the financing need grows faster than the available buffer.

### 3. Growth can deepen the cash hole

If every new sale requires materials and payroll before collection, each additional order needs cash first. A profitable unit economics model can therefore create a larger aggregate funding requirement as volume rises. The company has discovered product-market fit and a working-capital boss fight at the same time. The gaming reference is optional; the mechanism must remain clear without it.

This is the key escalation for the video: the problem is not confined to a weak company. Rapid growth can accelerate it.

### 4. Profit also omits or spreads major cash demands

Working capital is the cleanest teaching mechanism, but it is not the only route.

| Cash demand | Income-statement treatment | Why cash can fall faster than profit |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital expenditure** | The asset is usually capitalised; depreciation is recognised over its useful life | The equipment may be paid for now while only a fraction reaches the current period’s profit. |
| **Debt principal** | Principal repayment is not an expense; interest is | A large maturity can drain cash without reducing current-period operating profit by the same amount. |
| **Taxes** | Tax expense and cash tax can differ in timing | Prior-period liabilities, instalment schedules, and deferred tax accounting can separate expense from payment. |
| **Dividends / buybacks** | Distributions to owners, not operating expenses | They reduce cash without reducing net income. |
| **Legal settlements / provisions** | Recognition and payment may occur in different periods | The cash date can arrive after the expense—or before the final accounting estimate settles. |
| **Restricted cash** | May appear within broader cash disclosures but is not freely deployable | A headline cash number may overstate funds available for payroll or unsecured creditors. |

### 5. Refinancing determines whether timing becomes failure

A company with positive long-run economics may survive a temporary cash deficit by using:

- cash reserves;
- supplier credit;
- a revolving credit facility;
- receivables financing or factoring;
- an equity injection;
- asset sales;
- maturity extensions or covenant waivers.

Therefore, **an unpaid receivable does not cause bankruptcy by itself**. The decisive condition is that the funding gap exceeds cash and financing that is actually available on acceptable terms. Creditors may stop extending credit because collateral values fall, covenants are breached, a maturity cannot be refinanced, confidence disappears, or the underlying profits are no longer credible.

## Illustrative Numerical Model — Franklin’s Chair Company

This model is fictional and designed for animation. It assumes all revenue-recognition conditions are satisfied, customers pay 60 days after delivery, suppliers become payable immediately after each shipment, and taxes, overhead, defaults, and financing costs are ignored.

### Inputs

- Opening cash: **€250,000**
- Selling price per chair: **€150**
- cash cost / cost of goods sold per chair: **€100**
- customer payment term: **60 days**
- first shipment: **1,000 chairs on Day 1**
- second shipment: **2,000 chairs on Day 31**
- supplier payment: due upon each completed shipment

### Shipment 1 — Day 1

\[
\text{Revenue} = 1{,}000 \times €150 = €150{,}000
\]

\[
\text{Cost of goods sold} = 1{,}000 \times €100 = €100{,}000
\]

\[
\text{Profit} = €150{,}000 - €100{,}000 = €50{,}000
\]

The company pays €100,000 to its supplier. The customer owes €150,000, due on Day 61.

\[
\text{Cash after supplier payment} = €250{,}000 - €100{,}000 = €150{,}000
\]

### Shipment 2 — Day 31

Demand doubles. The company completes 2,000 chairs before the first customer payment arrives.

\[
\text{Revenue} = 2{,}000 \times €150 = €300{,}000
\]

\[
\text{Cost of goods sold} = 2{,}000 \times €100 = €200{,}000
\]

\[
\text{Profit} = €300{,}000 - €200{,}000 = €100{,}000
\]

The second supplier payment is €200,000, but only €150,000 is in the bank.

\[
\text{Funding gap} = €200{,}000 - €150{,}000 = €50{,}000
\]

### The contradiction

| Metric at Day 31 | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cumulative revenue recognised | €450,000 |
| Cumulative cost recognised | €300,000 |
| **Cumulative accounting profit** | **€150,000** |
| Receivables awaiting collection | €450,000 |
| Cash available before second supplier payment | €150,000 |
| Second supplier payment due | €200,000 |
| **Immediate funding gap** | **€50,000** |

The company is profitable in the model. It also cannot satisfy the Day 31 supplier payment without an overdraft, facility, extended terms, fresh equity, or another bridge. Its growth created more profit and a larger amount of cash waiting in receivables.

### Balance-sheet intuition

The receivables have value, so this is not necessarily balance-sheet insolvency. It is a timing problem. If the customer is creditworthy and a lender believes the invoices, bridge finance is plausible. If the customer disputes the invoices, the receivables are already pledged, the company has breached a covenant, or the lender refuses more exposure, the same timing problem becomes existential.

### Sensitivity

- If customers pay in 30 rather than 60 days, the first €150,000 arrives by Day 31 and removes the immediate gap.
- If suppliers grant 60-day terms, payables finance the operating cycle and the gap moves later.
- If opening cash is at least €300,000, both shipments can be paid before the first collection.
- If the second shipment is only 1,000 chairs, available cash of €150,000 covers the €100,000 supplier payment.
- If a committed facility provides at least €50,000 of accessible headroom, the company survives the illustrative date.

The takeaway is precise: **profitability creates eventual resources only if the revenue is collectible and the company can finance the interval.**

## Real-World Test — Carillion

### Why use it

Carillion gives the video authoritative public evidence for the warning signs: attractive reported margins, rising borrowing, weak working capital, cash-flow classification concerns, continued distributions, a sudden collapse in reported contract value, and failure to secure short-term funding. It is well documented in a joint House of Commons committee report and a House of Commons Library briefing.

### Why it is not a perfect example

Carillion should **not** be presented as a clean case of a genuinely profitable company that merely suffered unlucky payment timing. The post-collapse evidence raised serious doubts about revenue recognition, contract estimates, and the classification of supplier-finance borrowing. The safer formulation is:

> Carillion shows why a profitable income statement is not enough—and why poor cash conversion can be a warning that the profit itself deserves another look.

### Evidence chain

1. Until July 2017, audited accounts presented Carillion as profitable, with profit margins around **5–6%**. The parliamentary report also identified warning signs: borrowing rose from **£242 million in December 2009** to **£689 million in December 2016**, while the current ratio stayed around **1.0 from 2013 to 2016**. [House of Commons committees, paragraphs 76–78](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201719/cmselect/cmworpen/769/76905.htm)
2. Carillion reported **£509 million** of operating cash inflow against **£501 million** of group operating profit from 2013 to 2016. The committee cited analysis that reclassifying **£472 million** drawn through an early-payment facility as financing would leave only **£37 million** of cash-backed operating profit—a **7%** conversion rate. The accounting treatment depended on the detailed facility terms, and the Financial Reporting Council did not confirm the ratings agencies’ classification view. [House of Commons committees, paragraphs 91–94](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201719/cmselect/cmworpen/769/76905.htm)
3. A **£55 million** final dividend for 2016 was paid on 9 June 2017, one month before the July profit warning. From 2011 through 2016, Carillion paid **£441 million** of dividends and **£246 million** of pension-deficit recovery contributions. [House of Commons committees, paragraphs 17–18](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201719/cmselect/cmworpen/769/76905.htm)
4. On 10 July 2017, Carillion announced an **£845 million** contract write-down. A further **£200 million** was later added, wiping out seven years of previously reported profit, leaving **£405 million** of net liabilities, and taking borrowing to **£961 million**. The report said **£729 million** of previously recognised revenue was conceded to be unobtainable. [House of Commons committees, paragraphs 79–83](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201719/cmselect/cmworpen/769/76905.htm)
5. Carillion entered compulsory liquidation on **15 January 2018** after efforts to secure short-term financial support failed. A House of Commons Library briefing reported only **£29 million** of cash, more than **£1.3 billion** owed to banks, and a projected **£3.5 million** cash shortfall within days without further debt drawings. [House of Commons Library briefing, pages 7–8](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-8206/CBP-8206.pdf)

### What the case proves—and what it does not

**It supports:**

- reported profit can coexist with fragile liquidity;
- operating-cash-flow quality depends on working capital and classification;
- rising borrowing and weak cash conversion can matter more than a stable margin;
- distributions can consume cash while the income statement remains profitable;
- once lenders will not provide additional short-term support, the next cash deadline becomes decisive.

**It does not cleanly prove:**

- that Carillion’s historical profits were economically sound;
- that every company with low cash conversion is manipulating accounts;
- that a current ratio below a single universal threshold predicts collapse;
- that supplier finance is inherently improper;
- that dividends alone caused the failure.

## Optional Secondary Case — General Growth Properties

General Growth Properties (GGP) is useful if the script needs one brief US illustration of a maturity/refinancing crisis. Its 2010 Form 10-K says the company filed Chapter 11 in 2009 with significant past-due, imminently due, cross-collateralised or cross-defaulted debt. A total of 388 debtor entities with approximately **$21.83 billion** of debt filed. The reorganisation plans generally provided for allowed creditor claims to be paid in full, and old shareholders received equity in the reorganised GGP plus shares in Howard Hughes Corporation. [GGP 2010 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1496048/000104746911001829/a2202295z10-k.htm)

This supports two useful points:

- a legal bankruptcy process can be triggered by a debt-maturity/refinancing problem even when operating assets retain substantial value;
- bankruptcy does not always mean the business vanishes or every security becomes worthless.

Do **not** describe GGP as profitable at the filing date. Its reported results included major losses and impairments. It is a liquidity/refinancing example, not clean proof of the title by itself. For a 10-minute video, keep GGP in reserve; Carillion plus the fictional model is already enough.

## Jurisdiction and Legal Terminology

The title uses “bankrupt” in the ordinary global sense because it is legible and clickable. The narration should add one short qualification:

> The legal label depends on the country. In Britain, a company may enter administration or liquidation; in the United States, Chapter 11 usually means a court-supervised reorganisation. The shared economic problem is an obligation the company cannot fund on time.

In England and Wales, the official tests include inability to pay debts as they fall due and a balance-sheet test that includes contingent and prospective liabilities. [Insolvency Act 1986, section 123](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1986/45/section/123) and [UK government corporate-distress guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-sourcing-and-consultancy-playbooks/corporate-financial-distress-guidance-note-html)

In the United States, Chapter 11 is typically used to reorganise a business. The debtor normally continues operating as a debtor in possession and may seek court-approved financing. [US Courts, Chapter 11 Bankruptcy Basics](https://www.uscourts.gov/court-programs/bankruptcy/bankruptcy-basics/chapter-11-bankruptcy-basics)

Keep this to roughly 15–20 spoken seconds. The video is an accounting-and-finance explanation, not a comparative insolvency-law lecture.

## Warning Signs for an Investor or Manager

No single item proves distress. The useful discipline is to connect earnings, cash, and the payment calendar.

| Question | Signal to inspect | Why it matters | Benign alternative explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Are sales turning into cash? | CFO persistently below net income | Profit may be accumulating in working capital or estimates | Seasonal build, temporary customer timing, early growth |
| Are customers taking longer to pay? | Receivables or days sales outstanding rising faster than sales | More revenue is waiting for collection; credit quality may be weakening | Deliberate entry into a longer-term customer segment |
| Is inventory absorbing funding? | Inventory growth above sales; obsolescence reserves | Cash is committed before sale; markdown risk rises | Planned launch or supply-chain buffer |
| Is the company preserving cash by leaning on suppliers? | Payables days jump; supplier complaints; reverse factoring | Cash conversion may look stronger temporarily | Negotiated terms reflecting greater purchasing power |
| Does EBITDA overstate available cash? | Positive EBITDA, negative CFO or FCF | Working capital, capex, interest, and taxes still need cash | Deliberate high-return investment phase |
| Is there a payment cliff? | Near-term maturity wall, bullet repayment, limited facility headroom | Principal repayment is not captured in EBITDA and refinancing can disappear | Highly liquid issuer with committed refinancing |
| Can the company still borrow? | Tight covenants, waivers, collateral exhaustion, going-concern language | The bridge between profit and payment may be closing | Conservative early disclosure or temporary shock |
| Is cash being distributed despite weak conversion? | Dividends or buybacks alongside rising debt | Optional outflows reduce the buffer | Stable regulated or contracted cash flows |

The four most useful items for a basic-investing audience are:

1. compare net income with operating cash flow over several periods;
2. inspect receivables and inventory relative to sales;
3. subtract capital expenditure from CFO using a clearly stated FCF definition;
4. read the debt-maturity table and liquidity/covenant discussion.

## Strongest Counterarguments and Refinements

### Counterargument 1 — Timing gaps are ordinary, not bankruptcy

Correct. Most businesses routinely sell on credit, carry inventory, and refinance liabilities. Positive profit improves the chance of obtaining finance because it suggests eventual repayment capacity. The thesis should therefore avoid implying that receivables automatically cause failure.

**Refined claim:** A profitable company can become cash-flow insolvent when the timing gap exceeds cash and financing that remains available.

### Counterargument 2 — If profit never becomes cash, perhaps it was not real profit

Sometimes. Revenue recognition, provisions, impairment, contract estimates, and collectability judgements can make reported profit a poor guide to economics. Carillion illustrates this problem. But timing differences also arise under entirely normal, accurate accrual accounting. The fictional example proves the mechanism without relying on aggressive accounting.

**Refined claim:** Weak cash conversion can indicate normal growth financing, temporary timing, or weak profit quality. The explanation requires evidence, not a slogan.

### Counterargument 3 — A valuable solvent company can always borrow or sell assets

Not necessarily on the required date. Assets can be illiquid, pledged, specialised, disputed, or worth less in a forced sale. Creditors care about collateral, covenants, seniority, and confidence. A company may be balance-sheet solvent under one valuation and still unable to produce cash quickly enough.

### Counterargument 4 — “Bankrupt” is legally imprecise

Correct across jurisdictions. The title is acceptable as ordinary-language packaging only if the script distinguishes economic distress, insolvency tests, and legal proceedings briefly and accurately.

### Counterargument 5 — Negative free cash flow may be healthy

Correct. A company can invest heavily because attractive projects are available. The warning sign is not one negative period. It is the relationship between investment, expected returns, funding capacity, maturity dates, and repeated cash conversion.

## Visual and Production Notes

### Must-build visuals

1. **Opening contradiction:** +€150k cumulative profit vs €50k funding gap.
2. **Accrual timeline:** delivery today, cash on Day 61.
3. **Two-shipment ledger:** the main reusable animation.
4. **Working-capital cycle:** cash → inventory → sale → receivable → cash.
5. **Profit-to-cash bridge:** net income adjusted by working capital and non-cash items.
6. **Carillion evidence frames:** official report pages with exact, minimal highlights.
7. **Four-tab annual-report checklist:** CFO, working capital, capex, debt maturities.

### Avoid

- a generic “money flowing through pipes” animation without mapping each pipe to a balance-sheet item;
- stock footage of worried office workers as a substitute for the cash mechanism;
- showing Carillion workers or public-service consequences during jokes;
- presenting the current ratio as a universal bankruptcy threshold;
- using EBITDA as though it were a formal cash-flow subtotal;
- a second case study unless the first cut runs short.

### Humour opportunities

- After the working-capital reveal: “The company has found product-market fit. It has also unlocked the financing requirement.”
- On EBITDA: “A useful number. The bank remains stubbornly attached to the version that includes interest.”
- On supplier finance: “Changing the label can improve a cash-flow statement. It has less influence on the person expecting to be paid.”

Use at most two. The Carillion section should be dry and factual.

## Do Not Overclaim

- Do not say profit is “fake” because it is not cash.
- Do not say accrual accounting lets companies book any sale they want.
- Do not say fast growth automatically causes bankruptcy.
- Do not say a profitable company is legally insolvent whenever cash briefly turns negative.
- Do not say Carillion was a healthy profitable business undone only by payment timing.
- Do not say Carillion’s supplier-finance classification was definitively unlawful or fraudulent; the committee reported competing views and noted that detailed terms matter.
- Do not say a current ratio below 1.2 proves distress.
- Do not say negative free cash flow is inherently bad.
- Do not equate UK compulsory liquidation with US Chapter 11.
- Do not imply that shareholders normally retain value in bankruptcy; GGP was unusual and is an optional counterexample.

## Recommendations and Next Steps

1. **Approve the thesis and two-shipment model before scripting.** The numbers will anchor the narration and all core animations.
2. **Use Carillion as a cautionary test, not the premise’s sole proof.** The fictional model demonstrates a clean timing case; Carillion adds messier real-world evidence.
3. **Keep the legal qualification brief.** One sentence on jurisdiction preserves accuracy without derailing the accounting story.
