# Franklin — Voice Guide

## Target voice

Knowledgeable, conversational, precise, and dry. Franklin sounds like somebody who enjoys the mechanism enough to explain it clearly and distrusts any explanation that becomes suspiciously tidy.

The narration should feel written by a person with a point of view. It may be amused, skeptical, surprised, or occasionally blunt. It should never sound like a textbook summary wearing jokes as a hat.

## Sentence-level principles

- Write for the ear. Use contractions, fragments, and varied sentence length.
- Prefer concrete actors and verbs: “banks held,” “depositors withdrew,” “the central bank bought.”
- Introduce terminology after the viewer understands the problem it names.
- Put the important qualification beside the claim, not three paragraphs later.
- Allow a strong line to end without explaining its emotional significance.
- Use numbers comparatively. “$40 billion” becomes meaningful when paired with the relevant market, budget, population, or prior level.

## Common AI patterns to remove

Rewrite any line that relies on:

- “It is not X; it is Y” or close variants masquerading as insight.
- A one-word rhetorical question followed by its own answer: “The result? Panic.”
- Empty triplets of adjectives.
- Repeated section conclusions that restate the thesis.
- “Let that sink in,” “this is where X comes in,” “at its core,” “in today's world,” or “it is important to note.”
- Vague importance language such as “plays a crucial role” when the actual effect can be named.
- Empty transitions: “moreover,” “furthermore,” “additionally,” or “with that said.”
- Inflated words such as “pivotal,” “groundbreaking,” “transformative,” “multifaceted,” “robust,” “seamless,” “delve,” “unpack,” “landscape,” or “tapestry.”
- A conclusion that merely announces a “broader lesson” without identifying it.

Do not repair these by swapping synonyms. Rebuild the sentence around a concrete claim.

## Dry humour

Humour should usually emerge from one of four sources:

1. **Institutional understatement** — a formal term describing something plainly chaotic.
2. **Incentive collision** — each actor behaves rationally and the group produces nonsense.
3. **Scale mismatch** — a tiny assumption moves an absurd amount of money.
4. **Franklin's predicament** — Franklin watches later financial inventions use his face as moral support.

Keep punchlines short. Do not explain them, stack three jokes on one beat, or interrupt an emotional section to prove the video is funny.

Good tonal model:

> The contract called this a “liquidity event,” which is a dignified way of saying everybody wanted the same exit.

Weak tonal model:

> Buckle up, because this wild financial rollercoaster is about to get crazy.

## Gaming references

Gaming references are seasoning, not a parallel topic quota.

- Use one when a game mechanic provides a clean structural analogy: resource faucets and sinks, auction-house liquidity, item duplication, min-maxing, principal-agent problems, or coordination failures.
- State the useful mapping in one sentence so a non-player follows it.
- Prefer widely legible mechanics over proper-noun references.
- Do not force a reference into every video. Zero is a valid number.
- Do not use gaming slang as the explanation itself.
- Return to the financial mechanism quickly.

Example:

> A game economy can create gold faster than it deletes it. The numbers rise, old rewards lose purchasing power, and designers reach for new money sinks. A national economy is vastly more complicated, but the faucet-and-sink intuition is a useful first step.

## Technical explanations

Use this order when possible:

1. the practical problem;
2. the actors and their incentives;
3. the mechanism;
4. the formal term;
5. a numerical or historical example;
6. the limitation or counterexample.

Avoid analogy chains. One accurate model, tested against an example, is better than five clever comparisons.

## Delivery notation

Use sparingly:

- `[beat]` for one deliberate pause.
- `[hold]` for a rare longer silence.
- `[quick] ... [/quick]` for a short acceleration.
- `[slow] ... [/slow]` for a weighted reveal.
- *Italics* for the single word carrying vocal emphasis.
- `»` before the handful of lines the performance and edit must land.

Unmarked narration should already read naturally. Cues mark exceptions.

## Final voice test

Before delivery, read the draft aloud and ask:

- Does each paragraph add a new thought?
- Could this sentence belong to any finance video? If yes, make it specific or delete it.
- Did the joke reveal character or mechanism?
- Did a qualification become a vague hedge?
- Did the script circle a point because the next causal step was never researched?
- Would a smart viewer feel spoken with rather than spoken down to?
