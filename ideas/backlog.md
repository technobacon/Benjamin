# Franklin — Idea Bank

Every entry begins as a tension and an arguable thesis. “Inflation” is a subject. “Inflation fell, but prices did not” contains a viewer problem and can become a video.

The scores below assess **editorial potential**, not predicted views. Score each dimension from 1–5:

- **C — Curiosity:** strength of the contradiction, mystery, or click premise.
- **P — Payoff:** usefulness of the mental model the viewer gains.
- **T — Thesis:** clarity and strength of the central claim.
- **E — Evidence:** availability of authoritative sources and concrete examples.
- **V — Visuals:** ability to show the mechanism rather than merely narrate it.
- **F — Feasibility:** research and production fit for a solo creator.

## Banking, money, and crises

### America Had Enough Coins. It Still Ran Out.

- **Pillar:** Small event, big economic mechanism
- **Status:** researching — dossier and source ledger complete
- **Research dossier:** [research/2026-08-13-us-coin-circulation-disruption.md](../research/2026-08-13-us-coin-circulation-disruption.md)
- **Source ledger:** [research/2026-08-13-us-coin-circulation-source-ledger.md](../research/2026-08-13-us-coin-circulation-source-ledger.md)
- **Central question:** How can a country have billions of coins and still leave shops unable to make change?
- **Provisional thesis:** The 2020 problem was mainly a circulation failure, not an aggregate shortage: lockdowns interrupted the businesses and routines that normally move coins back through banks and into tills.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A coin shortage means the mint failed to produce enough metal discs.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Availability depends on flow as well as stock; an abundant object can become locally scarce when its distribution loop breaks.
- **Why somebody clicks:** A mundane pandemic sign—“exact change only”—becomes a clean lesson in circulation, logistics, and monetary plumbing.
- **Likely runtime:** 8–10 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Federal Reserve coin-distribution data, the U.S. Coin Task Force, Mint production, and contemporary retailer restrictions.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Coin production and regional allocation also mattered. The script should not replace the misleading phrase “coin shortage” with the equally simple claim that minting was irrelevant.
- **Visual anchor:** A fixed number of coins stops looping between homes, shops, banks, and the Federal Reserve.
- **Freshness risk:** Low
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F5 = **30/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open on shops asking for exact change while jars of coins sit untouched in millions of homes.
2. Separate the total stock of coins from the number arriving where transactions happen.
3. Map the normal circulation loop from customer to retailer to bank and back.
4. Break the loop with closed businesses, fewer cash transactions, and disrupted bank deposits.
5. Use Federal Reserve distribution and receipt data to show why the imbalance appeared quickly.
6. Explain how new mint production can help but cannot instantly rebuild a damaged logistics loop.
7. End with the broader model: shortages often occur at the wrong place and time, even when the thing itself still exists.


### Ireland Had No Banks for Six Months. The Economy Kept Working.

- **Pillar:** Historical event, reusable model
- **Status:** spark — historical documentary
- **Central question:** How did ordinary commerce continue during the long Irish bank strike of 1970?
- **Provisional thesis:** Cheques became informal circulating credit because local shopkeepers and publicans could judge customers’ trustworthiness; the economy improvised a decentralized payment system, although it was slower, unequal, and dependent on existing social knowledge.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Without clearing banks, a modern economy must quickly run out of usable money.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Money is partly a network of trusted claims. Communities can improvise that network for a while, but not without limits and hidden costs.
- **Why somebody clicks:** A six-month banking shutdown that did not produce the expected collapse sounds impossible and has an unusually human mechanism.
- **Likely runtime:** 11–14 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Antoin Murphy’s economic study of the Irish bank strikes, Central Bank and parliamentary records, strike dates, cheque use, and contemporary reporting.
- **Strongest counterargument:** The banks did not vanish completely: cash, post offices, foreign banks, delayed clearing, and prior relationships still mattered. “The economy kept working” must not become “nothing went wrong.”
- **Visual anchor:** Uncleared cheques pass through pubs and shops, accumulating endorsements like temporary banknotes.
- **Freshness risk:** Low
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E4 / V5 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Close the clearing banks on 1 May 1970 and ask how anyone pays by November.
2. Explain what cheque clearing normally does behind the scenes.
3. Show why cheques continued to circulate instead of being deposited immediately.
4. Put local publicans and shopkeepers in the role of informal credit assessors.
5. Explain how reputation and endorsements substituted for centralized verification.
6. Measure the frictions: uncertainty, unequal access, eventual bad cheques, and the reckoning when banks reopened.
7. End with the lesson that trust can decentralize payment, but only while people trust the future clearing event.


### Banks Don’t Lend Out Your Money

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — recommended first video
- **Central question:** Where does the money for a new bank loan come from?
- **Provisional thesis:** A bank normally creates a loan asset and matching deposit when it lends. Deposits still matter as funding and liquidity, but they are not a fixed pile distributed one-for-one to borrowers.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Banks collect savers’ money and pass the same money to borrowers.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Lending creates deposit money, while capital, liquidity, funding costs, regulation, credit risk, settlement, and borrower demand constrain the process.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title sounds wrong but challenges a genuinely common mental model.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Bank of England balance-sheet explanation of modern money creation.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Banks still compete intensely for deposits and funding, so “banks create money” must not be presented as “banks can lend without limits.”
- **Visual anchor:** A €300,000 mortgage expands both sides of a bank balance sheet.
- **Freshness risk:** Low, although jurisdiction-specific regulation must be checked.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Someone receives a €300,000 mortgage. Ask where that money existed the previous morning.
2. Present the familiar piggy-bank model of banking.
3. Show the loan asset and deposit liability appearing together.
4. Follow the deposit when the borrower pays somebody at another bank: reserves and settlement enter the story.
5. Explain the real constraints on lending.
6. Show how repayment destroys the deposit money previously created.
7. Resolve the objection that deposits still matter enormously without being lent out one-for-one.

### Why Everyone Ran on Silicon Valley Bank

- **Pillar:** Historical event, reusable model
- **Status:** spark — best developed after the bond video
- **Central question:** Why did rational depositors collectively make the bank impossible to save?
- **Provisional thesis:** SVB combined an interest-rate mismatch with concentrated uninsured deposits and unusually fast withdrawal coordination; confidence mattered because the underlying balance sheet gave depositors a reason to run.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A rumour or online panic suddenly destroyed an otherwise normal bank.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A run is a coordination problem built on maturity transformation, and digital banking removed much of the friction that once slowed it.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It converts a famous recent collapse into a comprehensible sequence of decisions.
- **Likely runtime:** 12–15 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** FDIC failure reports, balance-sheet disclosures, deposit concentration, security losses, and the withdrawal timeline.
- **Strongest counterargument:** The run should not be blamed solely on nervous depositors; management, supervision, asset-liability risk, and uninsured funding concentration created the vulnerability.
- **Visual anchor:** A slow balance-sheet build followed by an accelerating withdrawal counter.
- **Freshness risk:** Low for the historical case; use the latest completed official post-mortems.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F3 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open on the speed and scale of the withdrawals.
2. Explain SVB’s concentrated, largely uninsured business deposits.
3. Connect rising rates to losses on securities bought when yields were lower.
4. Show how asset sales crystallized losses and revealed the funding problem.
5. Model an individual depositor’s choice: wait or protect payroll now.
6. Explain how deposit insurance and central-bank liquidity try to change that payoff.
7. Conclude that smartphones did not invent bank runs; they accelerated a vulnerable bank’s deadline.

## Inflation, policy, and macroeconomics

### Brazil Beat Inflation With Money That Didn’t Exist

- **Pillar:** Economic history, policy mechanism
- **Status:** spark — flagship history candidate
- **Central question:** How did Brazil use a currency people could not hold to help end runaway inflation?
- **Provisional thesis:** The URV first created a relatively stable unit of account while payments still occurred in the rapidly depreciating cruzeiro real, helping coordinate prices and weaken backward-looking indexation before the physical real launched.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Stopping high inflation mainly requires printing a new banknote and removing zeros.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A monetary system has separate jobs—pricing, payment, and saving—and Brazil stabilized the pricing language before replacing the payment currency.
- **Why somebody clicks:** A successful anti-inflation plan built around “money” with no notes or coins is a strong, precise contradiction.
- **Likely runtime:** 12–15 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Banco Central do Brasil’s three-phase history of the Plano Real, URV conversion rules, inflation data, legislation, and accounts from plan designers.
- **Strongest counterargument:** The URV was not a magic trick. Fiscal measures, political credibility, exchange-rate choices, monetary policy, and later reforms were essential to the plan and its durability.
- **Visual anchor:** Price tags switch from a violently changing currency into a stable measuring ruler months before new cash appears.
- **Freshness risk:** Low
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F4 = **29/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open in early 1994 with prices changing so quickly that yesterday’s numbers are nearly useless.
2. Explain inflation inertia: contracts and prices rise because everyone expects and indexes to past inflation.
3. Separate money’s unit-of-account function from its means-of-payment function.
4. Introduce the URV: prices migrate to a stable daily unit while shoppers still pay in cruzeiros reais.
5. Show one purchase quoted in URV and converted into the payment currency at that day’s rate.
6. Add the fiscal, political, and exchange-rate supports that the simplified legend often omits.
7. Convert one URV into one real and end on the insight that Brazil first repaired the ruler used to measure prices.


### Inflation Fell. Prices Didn’t.

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — launch candidate
- **Central question:** Why can falling inflation coexist with a cost of living that still feels painfully high?
- **Provisional thesis:** Falling inflation describes a slower rate of price increase. Household recovery depends on the accumulated price level, income growth, and the household’s own spending basket.
- **Viewer begins believing:** If inflation falls, prices should go back toward where they started.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Disinflation slows further price increases but does not reverse the previous jump; affordability can improve through deflation, rising incomes, or both.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It resolves a common conflict between official data and lived experience.
- **Likely runtime:** 8–10 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** CPI level and year-on-year rate series paired with nominal and real wage data.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Broad CPI is an average and may genuinely understate or overstate an individual household’s experienced inflation.
- **Gaming reference, only if useful:** A currency’s inflation rate can fall even though an item never returns to its old grand-exchange price.
- **Visual anchor:** Inflation-rate bars falling while a price-level line continues rising.
- **Freshness risk:** Low if the mechanism is kept evergreen; medium if tied heavily to the latest release.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T4 / E5 / V5 / F5 = **29/30**

#### Short outline

1. Pair a celebratory inflation headline with a grocery receipt that remains expensive.
2. Separate the rate of change from the accumulated price level.
3. Demonstrate disinflation with a 100 → 110 → 113.3 numerical example.
4. Explain why personal consumption baskets differ from the published average.
5. Bring in nominal versus real wages and the idea of incomes catching up.
6. Explain why central banks usually target low positive inflation rather than reversing the entire price increase.
7. End with the precise promise and limitation of lower inflation.

### Governments Don’t Pay Off Debt Like You Do

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** Why do governments continually refinance debt instead of saving toward a final debt-free date?
- **Provisional thesis:** Sovereign debt sustainability depends on financing cost, maturity structure, currency, revenues, economic growth, and political credibility rather than on whether the nominal balance reaches zero.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A state should repay all debt like a household repays a mortgage.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Governments are continuing institutions that roll thousands of obligations, but refinancing capacity is not unlimited and can fail abruptly.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title challenges the most common analogy used in public debt debates.
- **Likely runtime:** 11–14 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** A government maturity profile, interest-to-revenue ratios, primary balances, and contrasting domestic- versus foreign-currency debt episodes.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Rejecting the household analogy must not become an excuse to treat debt service, inflation risk, currency risk, or political limits as irrelevant.
- **Visual anchor:** A wall of separate bonds maturing and being replaced rather than one giant national loan.
- **Freshness risk:** Low for the framework; any country example must be date-specific.
- **Potential score:** C4 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **27/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a government issuing new bonds to repay maturing bonds.
2. Explain that national debt is thousands of separate obligations with different maturities.
3. Contrast a finite household lifetime with a continuing state and tax base.
4. Introduce debt-to-GDP, interest expense, the primary balance, and the role of growth.
5. Explain how inflation changes nominal debt and real burden differently.
6. Show the danger points: short maturities, foreign currency, rising yields, and lost credibility.
7. End between the two bad analogies: sovereign debt is neither a household mortgage nor free money.

### Every Country Can’t Export Its Way to Prosperity

- **Pillar:** Economics hidden in ordinary policy arguments
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** Can every country improve its economy by exporting more than it imports?
- **Provisional thesis:** Export-led development can work for individual countries, but trade surpluses cannot be universal because global balances net to zero and mirror saving and investment flows.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Exports are economic wins and imports are economic losses, so every country should maximize the difference.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Exports can build productive capacity, but imports are also the goods and services a country receives; the balance alone does not reveal economic health.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It exposes a logical contradiction inside a popular political promise.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–13 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Global accounting identity, current-account and capital-account examples, and contrasting surplus and deficit countries.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Trade balances are not meaningless; their composition, financing, exchange-rate regime, and relationship to investment can create real vulnerabilities.
- **Visual anchor:** Every country tries to push export crates outward until the arrows have nowhere to land.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with several governments all promising to export more and import less.
2. Reframe exports as goods sent away and imports as goods received.
3. Establish why global trade balances must sum to zero.
4. Connect the current account to national saving, investment, and capital flows.
5. Show why neither a deficit nor surplus is automatically healthy or unhealthy.
6. Preserve the legitimate case for export-led development: productivity, scale, industrial learning, and foreign currency.
7. End with the constraint: every country can become more productive, but not every country can run a surplus against everyone else.

### Nobody Knows What Interest Rates Should Be

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — better after audience trust is built
- **Central question:** How can central banks set policy when the neutral interest rate cannot be observed?
- **Provisional thesis:** Policymakers control a nominal policy rate but must estimate the changing real rate that neither stimulates nor restrains demand from delayed and noisy economic evidence.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Central banks calculate the correct interest rate from a reasonably stable formula.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Monetary policy is an inference problem conducted in real time, and the supposedly neutral setting moves with the economy.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It undermines the image of a precise control panel without falling into “central banks know nothing” cynicism.
- **Likely runtime:** 11–14 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Central-bank estimates of neutral rates, real-rate calculations, and episodes where the stance of policy was misread.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Estimates can still guide policy even when uncertain; unobservable does not mean imaginary or useless.
- **Visual anchor:** Franklin operates an interest-rate dial whose neutral marker keeps moving.
- **Freshness risk:** Medium if using current neutral-rate estimates.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F3 = **27/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a central bank setting a rate without knowing the exact neutral level.
2. Define neutrality through its economic effect.
3. Separate nominal rates from real rates after expected inflation.
4. Explain why demographics, productivity, desired saving, and investment shift the neutral rate.
5. Show how policymakers infer it from inflation, employment, output, and financial conditions.
6. Examine the cost of an incorrect estimate in either direction.
7. End with an economy whose “neutral” label cannot be permanently printed on the dial.

## Personal finance and household decisions

### Your Emergency Fund Is Supposed to Lose Money

- **Pillar:** Personal finance through opportunity cost
- **Status:** spark — early personal-finance video
- **Central question:** Why hold cash that may trail inflation or markets?
- **Provisional thesis:** Emergency cash accepts lower expected return in exchange for liquidity, stability, and the ability to avoid expensive borrowing or forced asset sales at the worst moment.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Every idle euro should be invested because uninvested cash is wasted return.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** The return sacrificed on an emergency fund is partly an insurance premium; its success is measured by resilience when plans fail.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title makes a familiar financial virtue sound irrational, then gives the lost return a job.
- **Likely runtime:** 9–11 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Household emergency-savings data, borrowing costs, market drawdowns, and scenario comparisons between cash, credit, and forced selling.
- **Strongest counterargument:** “Supposed to lose” does not justify leaving cash in a needlessly poor account or choosing an arbitrary target. The required buffer depends on job stability, insurance, dependants, access to credit, and local safety nets.
- **Visual anchor:** Two timelines: one maximizes expected return; the other survives a job loss without selling investments during a drawdown.
- **Freshness risk:** Medium because savings rates, taxes, deposit protection, and products vary by country.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F5 = **29/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with cash earning less than a portfolio and ask why a rational investor would keep it.
2. Define the emergency fund’s actual mandate: immediate, reliable purchasing power.
3. Price the opportunity cost against the alternative costs of credit-card debt or a forced sale.
4. Run a job-loss scenario during a market decline.
5. Explain why liquidity and low volatility are valuable precisely when expected returns look unimpressive.
6. Reject one-size-fits-all month targets and identify the variables that change the buffer.
7. End by grading the fund on crises absorbed, not returns won.

### Investing Everything Today Usually Beats Waiting

- **Pillar:** Personal finance through probability
- **Status:** spark — early personal-finance video
- **Central question:** If someone already has a lump sum to invest, is it usually better to invest now or drip it into the market?
- **Provisional thesis:** Because risky assets have a positive expected return, immediate exposure has historically beaten a deliberate wait in cash more often than staged entry; averaging can still be rational when regret would otherwise prevent action or trigger panic selling.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Staging a lump sum is automatically safer and therefore the financially superior choice.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Staging reduces timing regret, not necessarily economic risk; the best plan must account for both expected return and the investor’s ability to stay invested.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It challenges one of the most comforting rituals in investing without pretending risk disappears.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Vanguard and academic lump-sum versus cost-averaging studies across markets, plus historical scenario analysis.
- **Strongest counterargument:** This applies to money already available, not to withholding regular savings while building a future lump sum. Horizon, allocation, valuation, tax, and behaviour can change the sensible implementation.
- **Visual anchor:** The same cash follows two paths: market exposure today versus a shrinking cash queue over twelve months.
- **Freshness risk:** Medium because implementation and tax wrappers are jurisdiction-specific.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F5 = **29/30**

#### Short outline

1. Give two investors the same already-available lump sum on the same date.
2. Explain why cash waiting on the sidelines has an expected-return cost.
3. Compare immediate and staged entry across many historical starting dates.
4. Show the cases where immediate entry loses badly and why those cases dominate memory.
5. Separate volatility from regret and expected return from emotional tolerability.
6. Clarify the crucial distinction between investing a windfall and investing each paycheck as it arrives.
7. End with a decision rule built around horizon, allocation, and the plan the investor can actually keep.

### The Mathematically Worse Way to Repay Debt Can Work Better

- **Pillar:** Behavioral personal finance
- **Status:** spark — personal-finance experiment
- **Central question:** Can paying the smallest balance first ever outperform paying the highest interest rate first?
- **Provisional thesis:** The avalanche method minimizes interest if behaviour is fixed, but small completed balances can create motivation and persistence; the snowball may improve follow-through for some borrowers even while charging a measurable financial premium.
- **Viewer begins believing:** There is one objectively correct repayment order, and anyone choosing differently is simply innumerate.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** The cheapest algorithm and the plan most likely to be completed can differ, so the real comparison includes both interest cost and adherence.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It pits clean mathematics against messy human behaviour without dismissing either.
- **Likely runtime:** 9–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Debt-account-aversion experiments, “small victories” research, household simulations, and a transparent numerical comparison.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Evidence of motivation does not make the snowball universally better; recent estimates find meaningful extra interest costs, especially for some lower-income households.
- **Gaming reference, only if useful:** The highest-DPS strategy is useless if the player quits the encounter after two attempts; keep the reference to one line.
- **Visual anchor:** Two debt bars race: one falls cheaper, the other produces earlier visible eliminations.
- **Freshness risk:** Low for the mechanism; avoid individualized debt advice.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E4 / V4 / F5 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Set up three debts where the smallest balance is not the highest-rate debt.
2. Prove why the avalanche minimizes interest under identical payments.
3. Introduce debt-account aversion and the satisfaction of closing an account.
4. Show experimental evidence for small victories and evidence for the extra interest cost.
5. Build the conditions under which motivation could outweigh the premium.
6. Offer hybrid approaches: capture an early win, then switch; automate payments; make interest visible.
7. End with a precise test: choose the cheapest plan you are genuinely likely to finish.

### Who Really Pays for Credit Card Rewards?

- **Pillar:** Personal finance hidden inside a two-sided market
- **Status:** spark — personal finance with distributional depth
- **Central question:** Where does the money for points, miles, and cashback actually come from?
- **Provisional thesis:** Rewards are funded through a mix of merchant fees, interest, annual fees, breakage, and changed spending; the gains and costs are distributed unevenly among merchants, revolvers, non-reward users, and disciplined reward users.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Cashback is free money supplied by the card network.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Rewards sit inside a pricing system with several payers, and “who funds whom” depends on merchant pass-through, cardholder behaviour, and market design.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It turns a pleasant 2% cashback notification into a forensic money trail.
- **Likely runtime:** 11–14 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Federal Reserve work on rewards redistribution and interchange, card issuer economics, merchant-fee data, and competing empirical interpretations.
- **Strongest counterargument:** The simple line “poor cash users subsidize rich card users” is too broad. Recent work emphasizes financial sophistication within income groups, and merchant pricing responses are difficult to identify cleanly.
- **Visual anchor:** A €100 card purchase splits into merchant revenue, network and issuer fees, rewards, interest, and unpaid balances.
- **Freshness risk:** Medium because card regulation and economics differ sharply by country.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Start with €2 cashback appearing after a €100 purchase.
2. Follow the transaction through merchant, acquirer, network, issuer, and customer.
3. Add interchange, network fees, annual fees, interest, and unredeemed points.
4. Show how merchants may spread payment costs through prices rather than itemize them.
