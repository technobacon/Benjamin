# Franklin Research Dossier — America Had Enough Coins. It Still Ran Out.

## Executive Summary

In the spring and summer of 2020, retailers across the United States began asking customers for exact change, steering them toward cards, rounding transactions, or replacing coin change with store credit. The natural explanation was that the country had not produced enough coins. Official evidence points to a different mechanism.

The United States already had more than $48 billion in coin in circulation. The problem was that a coin in a household jar, a closed bank lobby, or a shuttered coin-heavy business was unavailable to the cashier who needed it. The system depended much more heavily on recirculating existing coins than on minting new ones: in 2019, newly issued Mint coin represented only about 17% of the coins the Federal Reserve paid into the supply chain. Normally, the Reserve Banks received seven or eight coins back for every ten they distributed. During the acute disruption, the return fell to roughly four.

Lockdowns broke several return paths at once. Bank lobbies and retail locations closed; consumers made fewer small cash payments; coin-redemption kiosks received fewer deposits; and coin-heavy businesses such as laundromats, toll roads, transit systems, vending operations, casinos, and car washes slowed, closed, or moved toward digital payments. Mint production also fell temporarily as social-distancing measures were introduced. When retail activity began returning, demand for change recovered faster than coin deposits. The resulting gap reached as much as 3.5 billion coins in a month, depleted contingency inventories within weeks, and led the Federal Reserve to cap bank coin orders from 15 June 2020.

The Mint responded aggressively. It returned to full production in June, added mandatory overtime, and shipped 15.479 billion circulating coins in fiscal 2020—24.2% more than in fiscal 2019. This helped, but it could not quickly replace a recirculation network that normally supplied most of the flow. Conditions improved around August and September 2020, allocations were removed in January 2021, and a second reopening-driven imbalance forced allocations back in March 2021.

The strongest ten-minute video is therefore not a pandemic recap or a tour of the Mint. It is a compact economic mystery about **stock versus usable flow**. America possessed the coins in aggregate while the payment system ran short of the right denominations, in the right places, at the right time.

## Recommended Editorial Angle

The useful contradiction is precise:

> More than $48 billion in coins existed, but many retailers could not obtain enough working inventory to make change.

Do not spend the video asking whether coins physically existed. Resolve that within the first minute. The mystery is **why existing coins failed to arrive**.

The causal chain is:

1. Existing coins normally cycle through customers, coin-heavy services, aggregators, banks, armored carriers, Federal Reserve distribution points, and retailers.
2. New Mint output is only the top-up. In 2019, it represented about 17% of the coins paid into the supply chain.
3. Pandemic closures and payment changes interrupted the return side of the loop.
4. Retail demand for change recovered before deposits recovered.
5. Contingency inventories were thin and visibility across the fragmented system was poor.
6. The Federal Reserve rationed its remaining inventory while the Mint increased production.
7. Local shortages persisted despite national abundance because the coins were dormant, geographically misplaced, or outside accessible distribution channels.

The video should feel like a logistical mystery that happens to involve money. That makes it accessible without flattening the economics.

## Is the Title Accurate?

### “America had enough coins”

This is defensible if explained as an aggregate statement. On 23 July 2020, the U.S. Mint said there was “an adequate amount of coins in the economy.” The later U.S. Coin Task Force report cited more than $48 billion in coin already in circulation.

The figure needs two qualifications:

- It is a **dollar value**, not a count of pieces.
- “In circulation” includes coins that may sit dormant for long periods. It does not mean every coin was actively moving or available through banks.

### “It still ran out”

The country did not reach zero coins. Retailers, financial institutions, and Federal Reserve distribution locations ran short of usable inventories in particular denominations and locations. “Ran out” is the consumer-facing reality; “circulation imbalance” is the technically accurate mechanism.

A clean clarification near the start:

> The coins had not disappeared. The institutions expected to deliver them to cash registers were receiving far fewer coins back.

Avoid calling the event a national absence of coin. It was a national circulation disruption producing local and institutional shortages.

## Definitions That Must Stay Separate

| Term | Meaning in this story | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Aggregate coin stock** | The face value or number of U.S. coins estimated to remain in existence and circulation | A large total does not show whether coins are accessible or moving. |
| **Actively circulating coin** | Coins that still return through the payment system, even if only occasionally | This is narrower than all historically issued coin after estimated attrition. |
| **Flow** | Coins paid out or deposited during a period | Cash registers need continuing flow, not proof that coins exist somewhere. |
| **Inventory** | Coins held at a particular retailer, bank, carrier vault, Federal Reserve location, or Mint facility | A surplus in one node cannot automatically meet a shortage elsewhere. |
| **Federal Reserve payment** | Coin distributed from Reserve Bank or contracted coin-terminal inventory to a depository institution | These payments include both recirculated and newly minted coin. |
| **Federal Reserve receipt or deposit** | Coin returned by depository institutions into the Reserve Bank distribution system | The collapse in this return flow was the decisive problem. |
| **New coin shipment** | Coin supplied by the Mint to the Federal Reserve Banks | This is a top-up to the existing system, not the majority of normal circulation. |
| **Shortage** | Demand at a place and time exceeds obtainable supply | A local shortage can coexist with aggregate abundance. |
| **Circulation imbalance** | Coins pool in some locations while other locations cannot obtain enough | This is the most accurate short description of 2020. |
| **Denomination mix** | The combination of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters available | A till can have enough face value and still lack the pieces needed to make change. |

## How the Coin System Normally Works

### 1. The Mint makes new coins

The U.S. Mint is part of the Treasury and is the issuing authority for coins. Its Philadelphia and Denver facilities produce most circulating pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters. The Mint sells these coins to the Federal Reserve Banks at face value.

The Mint does not normally deliver rolls of quarters to a grocery store or a member of the public.

### 2. The Federal Reserve manages wholesale distribution

The Reserve Banks and contracted off-site locations hold and distribute coin for banks and credit unions. Much of the physical work is outsourced to coin terminals and depots operated by armored carriers. The 2022 Task Force report described roughly 175 outsourced locations and about ten carrier companies.

Depository institutions order coin through FedCash Services and deposit excess coin back into the system.

### 3. Banks and carriers connect the wholesale system to retailers

Banks and armored carriers supply tills, ATMs or coin equipment, small businesses, and large retailers. They also collect surplus coin and return it to distribution points.

### 4. Consumers and coin-heavy businesses complete the return loop

Consumers receive coin as change. Existing coins return through several routes:

- spending the coins in later transactions;
- depositing them at a bank or credit union;
- redeeming them at a coin-counting kiosk;
- paying at laundromats, vending machines, parking meters, tolls, transit systems, car washes, casinos, and other coin-intensive services;
- retailers and service providers depositing their accumulated coins through financial institutions.

This last section is easy to underestimate. A laundromat does not merely consume quarters. When it empties its machines and deposits those quarters, it becomes a large return pipe into the financial system.

## The Pre-Pandemic Equilibrium

Three figures establish the scale:

1. In 2019, the Federal Reserve paid roughly **68 billion coins** into circulation, worth about **$6.3 billion**.
2. Newly minted coin represented only about **17%** of the pieces paid into the supply chain.
3. In normal conditions, the Reserve Banks received about **seven or eight coins back for every ten paid out**.

The remaining two or three pieces did not cease to exist. They accumulated in jars, drawers, cup holders, collections, or other dormant locations. New Mint output replaced that leakage and supported growth in demand.

The combined Mint and Federal Reserve contingency buffer was only about **1.5 months of payable inventory**, according to the 2022 Task Force report. That was rational in a system with reasonably predictable returns, but fragile when deposits collapsed nationwide.

The important mental model is a reusable bottle system. Manufacturing new bottles matters, but daily availability mainly depends on old bottles returning.

## Why Coins Were Vulnerable Before 2020

The pandemic exposed weaknesses that already existed.

### Coins had become a one-way form of money

Consumers increasingly received coin as change but rarely used coin to initiate payment. The Task Force described coin as shifting from a fluid payment method into a low-value settlement asset. It was useful for completing a cash transaction, then often became inert.

### Redemption access was declining

Many bank branches had removed coin-counting machines, required customers to roll coins, refused loose coin, or limited exchange services to account holders. Consumer redemption increasingly depended on coin kiosks at retailers.

### The network had limited visibility

The Federal Reserve could observe its own orders, receipts, and inventory, but not all coin held by banks, carriers, aggregators, retailers, and consumers. Much coin moved through private “side flows” without returning to the Federal Reserve.

This matters during a shock. One region or institution may hoard inventory for precautionary reasons while another runs short, and there is no single live map showing where the coins are.

### Buffers were optimized for normal conditions

Low inventories reduced storage and handling costs. Coin is heavy, bulky, and expensive to count and transport relative to its value. Efficiency made sense until several return pathways failed simultaneously.

## What Broke in 2020

### Mechanism 1 — Coin deposits collapsed

After the pandemic was declared in March 2020, coin deposits from financial institutions to the Reserve Banks fell 40–50% below 2019 volumes, and as much as 60% for some denominations, according to the Task Force.

The later 2022 study put average monthly Federal Reserve coin deposits at approximately:

- **4.7 billion coins per month in 2019**
- **2.4 billion during the pandemic**

That is a decline of roughly 49%.

The largest causes were disruptions to consumer return paths:

- bank lobbies closed or reduced service;
- consumers visited stores and redemption kiosks less often;
- small cash transactions declined;
- coin-heavy service businesses stopped, slowed, or digitized;
- some consumers avoided physical payment because of early hygiene concerns.

Coinstar redemption reportedly reached a low around **60% below average in April 2020**, then recovered gradually and stabilized around 30% below average from September.

### Mechanism 2 — Mint production initially fell

Social distancing and employee-safety measures initially reduced Mint output by as much as 35%, according to the Task Force report. This contributed to the immediate squeeze.

It was not the dominant full-year explanation. Production returned to pre-pandemic levels around June, mandatory overtime was introduced, and fiscal-year shipments ultimately increased sharply.

### Mechanism 3 — Demand returned faster than supply

Bank orders initially fell when the economy closed. As regions reopened in late spring and summer, retailers again needed change. Orders from banks returned toward pre-pandemic levels while customer coin deposits remained weak.

The acute monthly gap between desired coin and available supply reached as much as **3.5 billion coins**.

During this period, the return ratio fell to approximately:

- **four coins received for every ten paid out**
- versus **seven or eight received for every ten** before the pandemic.

Contingency inventories at the Mint and Federal Reserve were depleted within weeks.

### Mechanism 4 — Precautionary behavior and poor visibility added friction

Banks and retailers could not be certain when their next delivery would arrive, so holding additional inventory was individually rational. The Task Force later estimated that financial-institution inventory increases of two to three billion pieces were materially smaller than the 15–25 billion-piece decline associated with eroding consumer return paths. Hoarding was therefore an amplifier, not the main cause.

This distinction matters. Blaming irrational panic would produce a simpler story and a worse explanation.

## Numerical Reconciliation

### Calendar-year Federal Reserve flows

The Federal Reserve reported:

- 51.9 billion coins distributed in calendar 2020;
- 34.0 billion coins received.

The annual return ratio was therefore:

[
34.0 div 51.9 approx 65.5%
]

This does not contradict the Task Force’s “four returned for every ten paid” description. The 40% figure describes the acute disrupted period; the 65.5% figure averages the entire calendar year, including months before the deepest disruption and months of partial recovery.

Using the reported year-over-year declines:

[
2019 payments approx 51.9 div (1 - 0.241) = 68.4	ext{ billion}
]

[
2019 receipts approx 34.0 div (1 - 0.394) = 56.1	ext{ billion}
]

These reconstructed figures align with the Task Force’s rounded statement that roughly 68 billion coins were paid out in 2019.

### The Mint’s response

Fiscal-year shipments rose from 12.466 billion to 15.479 billion:

[
(15.479 - 12.466) div 12.466 approx 24.2%
]

This is a real production response, but the fiscal-year measurement ends on 30 September and should not be subtracted directly from calendar-year Federal Reserve flows.

### Production by denomination in FY2020

The Mint’s total shipments consisted approximately of:

| Denomination | Pieces | Share of total pieces | Face value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Penny | 8.174 billion | 52.8% | $81.74 million |
| Nickel | 1.598 billion | 10.3% | $79.90 million |
| Dime | 2.801 billion | 18.1% | $280.10 million |
| Quarter | 2.906 billion | 18.8% | $726.50 million |
| **Total** | **15.479 billion** | **100%** | **approximately $1.168 billion** |

The piece count is dominated by pennies, while most face value comes from quarters. This is useful if the video briefly explains why “make more coins” also requires choosing which denominations to prioritize.

## Why the Mint Could Not Simply Fix It

### Most normal supply was old coin

If new coin provided about 17% of the 2019 flow, even a large percentage increase in Mint output addressed only a minority source.

A rough illustration:

- 17 units normally come from new coin;
- 83 come from recirculation;
- if recirculation loses half its flow, increasing the 17 by 25% adds only about four units.

This is not an exact reconstruction of 2020. It is a scale illustration showing why a dramatic factory response can remain smaller than a network failure.

### Production takes time

Metal, blanks, labor, dies, presses, packaging, transport, and denomination planning cannot adjust instantly. Safety measures initially constrained staffing.

### A newly made coin still needs distribution

A quarter leaving Denver is not yet a quarter in a local supermarket till. It must move through Federal Reserve inventory, terminals, banks, carriers, and the retailer.

### The wrong denomination is still a shortage

A store needing quarters cannot solve the problem with an equal face value of pennies. Production capacity devoted to one denomination cannot be switched without operational trade-offs.

### Dormant coins remain legal money

Unlike perishable goods, old coins do not expire. The problem was access and incentive: people had little reason to carry, spend, roll, or redeem low-value pieces.

## Why Demand for Coins Persisted While Cash Use Fell

This is the point most likely to confuse an attentive viewer.

Cash use dropped sharply in 2020: the Federal Reserve’s Diary found that cash represented 19% of consumer payments, down from 26% in 2019. Fewer cash transactions should reduce demand for change.

It did. The problem was that **recirculated supply fell even faster**.

Remaining cash users still needed complete transactions. A customer paying $10 for a $9.67 purchase requires 33 cents of change. One note arriving does not replenish the quarter, nickel, and pennies leaving the till. A small minority of cash transactions can therefore continue draining coin inventory if customers rarely pay with coins and return channels are weak.

Demand was also uneven:

- grocery and convenience retail remained active;
- lower-income and unbanked customers remained more cash-dependent;
- some coin-intensive services reopened at different speeds;
- self-checkout equipment can require inventory at many separate machines;
- denomination needs vary by business and pricing.

Aggregate transaction volume alone could not show where the shortage would appear.

## Real Consequences

### Retailers changed how they made change

Reported responses included:

- exact-change requests;
- card-only registers;
- rounding transactions down;
- asking customers to round up for charity;
- placing residual change on loyalty cards;
- issuing vouchers;
- offering incentives for customers to bring coins.

Kroger’s July 2020 response is a clean example: some stores applied coin change to loyalty cards or offered donation rather than handing over coins.

### Cash users carried the larger burden

A card user could usually switch payment methods. In 2019, 7.1 million U.S. households had no bank account. Cash-reliant consumers could face exact-change requirements or be turned away.

This gives the story stakes without inflating the crisis. Surveys found that most cash users still reported receiving change, so do not claim that cash stopped functioning nationally. The impact was intermittent, local, and disproportionately inconvenient for people with fewer alternatives.

### Retailers absorbed costs

Businesses spent staff time searching for coin, reconfiguring registers, moving inventory, arranging alternative supply, and paying card-processing fees when customers shifted away from cash. Some coin-dependent businesses invested in app-based payment systems.

## The Response and Why the Problem Returned

### Federal Reserve allocation

Effective 15 June 2020, the Reserve Banks capped coin orders by denomination and institution using historical order volumes and current Mint production. This did not create the shortage; it rationed scarce wholesale inventory more evenly.

### Higher Mint output and reserve release

The Mint raised production, while the Mint and Federal Reserve released roughly five billion pieces from reserves over two to three months, according to the later joint report.

### Public recirculation campaign

The Mint and Task Force asked consumers to:

- use exact change;
- spend stored coins;
- deposit or exchange coins at financial institutions;
- use coin-redemption kiosks.

These were not public-relations decorations. The system genuinely required coin to move out of private storage.

### Temporary improvement

Deposits improved and orders slowed around August and September 2020. The first allocation wave ended in January 2021.

### Second wave

Reopening demand accelerated again in March and April 2021 while deposits weakened, and the Federal Reserve reintroduced allocations. The recurrence supports the stronger thesis: increased Mint production treated the inventory gap, but the return network remained impaired.

## Broader Economic Lessons

### 1. Shortages are about accessible supply

An object can exist and still be unavailable at the location and time of demand. This applies to shipping containers, hospital beds, electricity capacity, housing, skilled labor, and computer chips as well as coins.

### 2. Stocks and flows answer different questions

The total stock tells us how much exists. Flow tells us how much becomes available during a period. A cash register is replenished by flow.

### 3. Location and form are economic properties

A billion pennies are not a substitute for the required quarters. Coins in a household jar are not equivalent to wrapped rolls inside an armored-carrier terminal near a bank.

### 4. Efficient systems can be fragile

Thin inventories and optimized return networks reduce normal costs. They also make simultaneous disruption more damaging. The coin network carried roughly 1.5 months of payable inventory because deposits were normally dependable.

### 5. Decentralized systems can hide their own inventory

Cash is anonymous and coins move through thousands of institutions. That privacy and decentralization make a real-time inventory map difficult. During stress, limited visibility encourages precautionary behavior and slows redistribution.

### 6. Declining use can destabilize a network before demand disappears

Coin use was shrinking, but millions of people still depended on cash. As return pathways eroded, the remaining cash system became harder to operate. A payment method can become less common and more fragile at the same time.

## Competing Explanations

| Explanation | Evidence for it | Limitation | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Mint stopped producing enough coins** | Output initially fell by as much as 35% under safety measures | Production recovered by June and FY2020 shipments rose 24.2%; new coin was a minority of normal flow | Contributing trigger, not primary full-year cause |
| **Consumers hoarded coins** | Coins accumulated in jars and redemptions fell | “Hoarding” often implies panic; much of the behavior was passive and rational because redemption was inconvenient | Important mechanism, better described as dormancy |
| **Banks hoarded inventory** | Some institutions held more coin because future supply was uncertain | Later estimates put FI inventory growth far below the decline in consumer recirculation | Amplifier, not main cause |
| **Cash use collapsed** | Cash share and small in-person payments fell sharply | Retail demand for change fell less than the return supply of coin | Part of the explanation because it broke return paths, not a complete answer |
| **The Federal Reserve rationed coin and caused the problem** | Banks received capped orders from 15 June | Caps followed depleted inventory and were intended to distribute it equitably | Response, not original cause |
| **There was no real shortage because coins existed** | More than $48 billion existed in aggregate | Retailers and institutions genuinely lacked obtainable inventory | Semantic objection; local shortage and aggregate abundance can coexist |
| **Fear of virus transmission through cash caused the disruption** | Early contact concerns encouraged touchless payments | The relative contribution is hard to measure; closures and access changes were independently important | Contributing behavioral factor, not proven dominant cause |

### Compress

### Avoid

- treating the event as a literal nationwide absence of coins;
- saying Americans suddenly decided to hoard coins in panic;
- implying the Mint was idle or incompetent;
- calling the Federal Reserve the issuer of U.S. coins;
- mixing fiscal-year Mint shipments with calendar-year Federal Reserve flows as if they share an exact period;
- describing $48 billion as 48 billion coins;
- claiming every coin “in circulation” was active and usable;
- saying stores legally could not accept cash without coins; laws vary by jurisdiction;
- saying the problem was completely solved in late 2020;
- claiming virus transmission through coins was a major proven cause;
- using the 2022 household-jar estimates as audited national inventory.

### Suggested ending direction

Avoid a grand speech about “the true nature of money.” Return to the small physical object:

- Retail response cards: exact change, loyalty credit, round down, donate, card.

## Primary and Institutional Source Guide

1. [U.S. Coin Task Force — The State of Coin](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/state-coin-6450/fulltext)  
   Best source for normal circulation mechanics, the acute chronology, the return ratios, the 3.5-billion monthly gap, allocation waves, and stakeholder impacts. It explicitly describes itself as an informed industry summary rather than academic research.

2. [U.S. Mint and Federal Reserve — U.S. Coin Circulation: The Path Forward](https://www.frbservices.org/binaries/content/assets/crsocms/financial-services/cash/101322-us-coin-supply-chain-final-report.pdf)  
   Best later diagnosis of root causes. Based on interviews, a 5,000-person survey, stakeholder data, and commissioned analysis. Some underlying proprietary data are not public.

3. [Federal Reserve 2020 Annual Report — Currency and Coin](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2020-payment-system-and-reserve-bank-oversight.htm)  
   Primary source for calendar-2020 Federal Reserve payments and receipts: 51.9 billion paid and 34.0 billion received.

4. [U.S. Mint — July 2020 Statement on Circulating Coins](https://www.usmint.gov/news/press-releases/statement-on-circulating-coins)  
   Contemporary official explanation that the economy had adequate aggregate coin and a circulation problem; also states 2019’s 17% new-coin contribution and June production response.

5. [U.S. Mint 2020 Annual Report](https://www.usmint.gov/content/dam/usmint/reports/2020-Annual-Report.pdf)  
   Primary source for fiscal-year shipments, denomination totals, costs, and the 24.2% increase.

6. [St. Louis Fed — How Do Coins Get to Where They’re Needed?](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2020/september/how-coins-get-where-needed)  
   Accessible official explanation of institutional roles, June allocation, task-force response, and why consumers should not take coins directly to a Reserve Bank.

7. [San Francisco Fed — 2020 Diary of Consumer Payment Choice summary](https://www.frbsf.org/news-and-media/news-articles/press-releases/2021/05/consumer-payment-behavior-reflects-pandemic-uncertainty/)  
   Primary institutional summary for the fall in small payments, in-person payments, and cash’s share of transactions.

8. [FDIC — How America Banks: 2019 Survey](https://www.fdic.gov/household-survey/how-america-banks-household-use-banking-and-financial-services)  
   Primary source for the 5.4% and 7.1-million unbanked-household estimates.

## Research Handoff

### Verified

- The U.S. had an adequate aggregate stock of coin during the 2020 disruption.
- Existing recirculated coin supplied most normal Federal Reserve coin payments.
- Pandemic restrictions and payment changes severely reduced coin deposits.
- Retail coin demand recovered faster than deposits.
- The Federal Reserve began temporary allocations on 15 June 2020.
- Mint fiscal-year shipments increased 24.2% to 15.479 billion.
- The first imbalance improved in late 2020 and returned during 2021 reopening.
- Cash-reliant consumers had fewer substitutes when retailers requested cards or exact change.

### Contested or measurement-sensitive

- The exact contribution of hygiene concerns relative to closures and digital substitution.
- The amount of precautionary inventory held by individual banks and retailers.
- The precise national value of coins inside household jars.
- Whether post-pandemic return pathways would ever fully recover to the old equilibrium.
- The strongest causal claims based on proprietary Task Force survey and stakeholder data.

### Unresolved

- A complete denomination-by-denomination daily series for the acute 2020 shortage.
- A reliable national dataset showing which retailers or regions ran out first.
- The exact end date of every Federal Reserve denomination cap after the second wave.
- The counterfactual duration of the disruption without higher Mint production.

### Do not say

- “The United States had 48 billion coins.”
- “The Mint stopped making coins.”
- “Americans panicked and hoarded all the change.”
- “The Federal Reserve printed more coins.”
- “Cash disappeared during the pandemic.”
- “The coin shortage ended in 2020.”
- “The country literally had no coins.”
- “Making more coins had no effect.”
