# Benjamin — Idea Bank

Every entry begins as a tension and an arguable thesis. “Inflation” is a subject. “Inflation fell, but prices did not” contains a viewer problem and can become a video.

The scores below assess **editorial potential**, not predicted views. Score each dimension from 1–5:

- **C — Curiosity:** strength of the contradiction, mystery, or click premise.
- **P — Payoff:** usefulness of the mental model the viewer gains.
- **T — Thesis:** clarity and strength of the central claim.
- **E — Evidence:** availability of authoritative sources and concrete examples.
- **V — Visuals:** ability to show the mechanism rather than merely narrate it.
- **F — Feasibility:** research and production fit for a solo creator.

## Banking, money, and crises

### Banks Don’t Lend Out Your Money

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — recommended first video
- **Central question:** Where does the money for a new bank loan come from?
- **Provisional thesis:** A bank normally creates a loan asset and matching deposit when it lends. Deposits still matter as funding and liquidity, but they are not a fixed pile distributed one-for-one to borrowers.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Banks collect savers’ money and pass the same money to borrowers.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Lending creates deposit money, while capital, liquidity, funding costs, regulation, credit risk, settlement, and borrower demand constrain the process.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title sounds wrong but challenges a genuinely common mental model.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Bank of England balance-sheet explanation of modern money creation.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Banks still compete intensely for deposits and funding, so “banks create money” must not be presented as “banks can lend without limits.”
- **Visual anchor:** A €300,000 mortgage expands both sides of a bank balance sheet.
- **Freshness risk:** Low, although jurisdiction-specific regulation must be checked.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Someone receives a €300,000 mortgage. Ask where that money existed the previous morning.
2. Present the familiar piggy-bank model of banking.
3. Show the loan asset and deposit liability appearing together.
4. Follow the deposit when the borrower pays somebody at another bank: reserves and settlement enter the story.
5. Explain the real constraints on lending.
6. Show how repayment destroys the deposit money previously created.
7. Resolve the objection that deposits still matter enormously without being lent out one-for-one.

### Why Everyone Ran on Silicon Valley Bank

- **Pillar:** Historical event, reusable model
- **Status:** spark — best developed after the bond video
- **Central question:** Why did rational depositors collectively make the bank impossible to save?
- **Provisional thesis:** SVB combined an interest-rate mismatch with concentrated uninsured deposits and unusually fast withdrawal coordination; confidence mattered because the underlying balance sheet gave depositors a reason to run.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A rumour or online panic suddenly destroyed an otherwise normal bank.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A run is a coordination problem built on maturity transformation, and digital banking removed much of the friction that once slowed it.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It converts a famous recent collapse into a comprehensible sequence of decisions.
- **Likely runtime:** 12–15 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** FDIC failure reports, balance-sheet disclosures, deposit concentration, security losses, and the withdrawal timeline.
- **Strongest counterargument:** The run should not be blamed solely on nervous depositors; management, supervision, asset-liability risk, and uninsured funding concentration created the vulnerability.
- **Visual anchor:** A slow balance-sheet build followed by an accelerating withdrawal counter.
- **Freshness risk:** Low for the historical case; use the latest completed official post-mortems.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F3 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open on the speed and scale of the withdrawals.
2. Explain SVB’s concentrated, largely uninsured business deposits.
3. Connect rising rates to losses on securities bought when yields were lower.
4. Show how asset sales crystallized losses and revealed the funding problem.
5. Model an individual depositor’s choice: wait or protect payroll now.
6. Explain how deposit insurance and central-bank liquidity try to change that payoff.
7. Conclude that smartphones did not invent bank runs; they accelerated a vulnerable bank’s deadline.

## Inflation, policy, and macroeconomics

### Inflation Fell. Prices Didn’t.

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — launch candidate
- **Central question:** Why can falling inflation coexist with a cost of living that still feels painfully high?
- **Provisional thesis:** Falling inflation describes a slower rate of price increase. Household recovery depends on the accumulated price level, income growth, and the household’s own spending basket.
- **Viewer begins believing:** If inflation falls, prices should go back toward where they started.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Disinflation slows further price increases but does not reverse the previous jump; affordability can improve through deflation, rising incomes, or both.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It resolves a common conflict between official data and lived experience.
- **Likely runtime:** 8–10 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** CPI level and year-on-year rate series paired with nominal and real wage data.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Broad CPI is an average and may genuinely understate or overstate an individual household’s experienced inflation.
- **Gaming reference, only if useful:** A currency’s inflation rate can fall even though an item never returns to its old grand-exchange price.
- **Visual anchor:** Inflation-rate bars falling while a price-level line continues rising.
- **Freshness risk:** Low if the mechanism is kept evergreen; medium if tied heavily to the latest release.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T4 / E5 / V5 / F5 = **29/30**

#### Short outline

1. Pair a celebratory inflation headline with a grocery receipt that remains expensive.
2. Separate the rate of change from the accumulated price level.
3. Demonstrate disinflation with a 100 → 110 → 113.3 numerical example.
4. Explain why personal consumption baskets differ from the published average.
5. Bring in nominal versus real wages and the idea of incomes catching up.
6. Explain why central banks usually target low positive inflation rather than reversing the entire price increase.
7. End with the precise promise and limitation of lower inflation.

### Governments Don’t Pay Off Debt Like You Do

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** Why do governments continually refinance debt instead of saving toward a final debt-free date?
- **Provisional thesis:** Sovereign debt sustainability depends on financing cost, maturity structure, currency, revenues, economic growth, and political credibility rather than on whether the nominal balance reaches zero.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A state should repay all debt like a household repays a mortgage.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Governments are continuing institutions that roll thousands of obligations, but refinancing capacity is not unlimited and can fail abruptly.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title challenges the most common analogy used in public debt debates.
- **Likely runtime:** 11–14 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** A government maturity profile, interest-to-revenue ratios, primary balances, and contrasting domestic- versus foreign-currency debt episodes.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Rejecting the household analogy must not become an excuse to treat debt service, inflation risk, currency risk, or political limits as irrelevant.
- **Visual anchor:** A wall of separate bonds maturing and being replaced rather than one giant national loan.
- **Freshness risk:** Low for the framework; any country example must be date-specific.
- **Potential score:** C4 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **27/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a government issuing new bonds to repay maturing bonds.
2. Explain that national debt is thousands of separate obligations with different maturities.
3. Contrast a finite household lifetime with a continuing state and tax base.
4. Introduce debt-to-GDP, interest expense, the primary balance, and the role of growth.
5. Explain how inflation changes nominal debt and real burden differently.
6. Show the danger points: short maturities, foreign currency, rising yields, and lost credibility.
7. End between the two bad analogies: sovereign debt is neither a household mortgage nor free money.

### Every Country Can’t Export Its Way to Prosperity

- **Pillar:** Economics hidden in ordinary policy arguments
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** Can every country improve its economy by exporting more than it imports?
- **Provisional thesis:** Export-led development can work for individual countries, but trade surpluses cannot be universal because global balances net to zero and mirror saving and investment flows.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Exports are economic wins and imports are economic losses, so every country should maximize the difference.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Exports can build productive capacity, but imports are also the goods and services a country receives; the balance alone does not reveal economic health.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It exposes a logical contradiction inside a popular political promise.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–13 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Global accounting identity, current-account and capital-account examples, and contrasting surplus and deficit countries.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Trade balances are not meaningless; their composition, financing, exchange-rate regime, and relationship to investment can create real vulnerabilities.
- **Visual anchor:** Every country tries to push export crates outward until the arrows have nowhere to land.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with several governments all promising to export more and import less.
2. Reframe exports as goods sent away and imports as goods received.
3. Establish why global trade balances must sum to zero.
4. Connect the current account to national saving, investment, and capital flows.
5. Show why neither a deficit nor surplus is automatically healthy or unhealthy.
6. Preserve the legitimate case for export-led development: productivity, scale, industrial learning, and foreign currency.
7. End with the constraint: every country can become more productive, but not every country can run a surplus against everyone else.

### Nobody Knows What Interest Rates Should Be

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — better after audience trust is built
- **Central question:** How can central banks set policy when the neutral interest rate cannot be observed?
- **Provisional thesis:** Policymakers control a nominal policy rate but must estimate the changing real rate that neither stimulates nor restrains demand from delayed and noisy economic evidence.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Central banks calculate the correct interest rate from a reasonably stable formula.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Monetary policy is an inference problem conducted in real time, and the supposedly neutral setting moves with the economy.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It undermines the image of a precise control panel without falling into “central banks know nothing” cynicism.
- **Likely runtime:** 11–14 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Central-bank estimates of neutral rates, real-rate calculations, and episodes where the stance of policy was misread.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Estimates can still guide policy even when uncertain; unobservable does not mean imaginary or useless.
- **Visual anchor:** Benjamin operates an interest-rate dial whose neutral marker keeps moving.
- **Freshness risk:** Medium if using current neutral-rate estimates.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F3 = **27/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a central bank setting a rate without knowing the exact neutral level.
2. Define neutrality through its economic effect.
3. Separate nominal rates from real rates after expected inflation.
4. Explain why demographics, productivity, desired saving, and investment shift the neutral rate.
5. Show how policymakers infer it from inflation, employment, output, and financial conditions.
6. Examine the cost of an incorrect estimate in either direction.
7. End with an economy whose “neutral” label cannot be permanently printed on the dial.

## Markets and investing

### How a “Safe” Bond Loses 20% Without Missing a Payment

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — launch candidate
- **Central question:** How can a bond make every promised payment and still inflict a large market loss?
- **Provisional thesis:** When market yields rise, the price of existing fixed cash flows must fall until they compete with newly issued bonds; longer-duration cash flows require the larger adjustment.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A safe bond should keep its value unless the issuer may default.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Credit safety and price stability are different properties; interest-rate risk can be large even when default risk is negligible.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title combines “safe,” “20% loss,” and “no missed payment” in one defensible contradiction.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** A simple fixed-rate government bond repriced against a higher-yield new issue, followed by a real bond-index example.
- **Strongest counterargument:** A holder who can wait until maturity may receive every contractual cash flow, although opportunity cost and liquidity needs remain real.
- **Visual anchor:** A fixed stack of future cash payments visibly shrinks in present value as the discount rate rises.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F4 = **29/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a highly rated bond losing roughly 20% despite making its payments.
2. Build an old 2% bond and a comparable new 5% bond.
3. Reduce the old bond’s price until its return becomes competitive.
4. Explain why distant fixed payments react more strongly: duration without a formula dump.
5. Separate credit risk from interest-rate risk.
6. Handle the hold-to-maturity objection honestly.
7. End by setting up why a bank holding “safe” securities might still face a liquidity problem.

### Good News Can Crash a Stock

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** Why can record revenue or profit be followed by a falling share price?
- **Provisional thesis:** A stock reacts to new information relative to what its price already implied, not to whether a headline is positive in isolation.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Better company results should mechanically produce a higher share price.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A strong business result can be a negative investment surprise when expectations, guidance, valuation, or discount rates were even more demanding.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It explains a market reaction that repeatedly appears irrational to casual observers.
- **Likely runtime:** 8–10 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Earnings surprises, guidance changes, analyst expectations, and a simple valuation example.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Short-term price moves can contain noise and positioning effects; not every post-earnings fall is a clean expectations calculation.
- **Visual anchor:** An earnings bar rises while a taller expectations bar is revealed behind it.
- **Freshness risk:** Low with durable examples; medium if tied to current earnings.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E4 / V4 / F5 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with record results and a falling share price.
2. Establish that the prior price already contained an expectation.
3. Build a numerical example: earnings grow 10% against an expected 20%.
4. Explain why forward guidance can outweigh the completed quarter.
5. Add valuation and discount rates: improving cash flows can still become less valuable today.
6. Acknowledge market noise and positioning.
7. End by separating a good company from a good investment at a particular price.

### Oil Was Worth Less Than Nothing

- **Pillar:** Historical event, reusable market mechanism
- **Status:** spark — high-click documentary
- **Central question:** How did an oil futures contract settle at a negative price?
- **Provisional thesis:** The May 2020 WTI contract became a delivery obligation near expiry during collapsing demand and severe storage constraints, making escape from the contract valuable enough for sellers to pay buyers.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A negative oil price meant physical oil everywhere had become worthless.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** One expiring futures contract briefly carried a costly physical obligation under exceptional market and storage conditions.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The premise appears economically impossible and has a precise historical payoff.
- **Likely runtime:** 9–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** CFTC report on the May 2020 WTI contract, expiry mechanics, Cushing delivery, storage constraints, and the –$37.63 settlement.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Storage pressure alone may not fully explain the extreme settlement; liquidity, trading behaviour, contract structure, and convergence problems require careful treatment.
- **Visual anchor:** A quoted oil price crosses zero while a warehouse gauge hits full.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P4 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with sellers paying buyers to take oil exposure.
2. Reveal that the price belonged to a specific expiring futures contract.
3. Explain futures expiry and physical delivery at Cushing.
4. Show the pandemic demand collapse and limited storage capacity.
5. Explain why traders unable to receive oil needed to exit at almost any price.
6. Cover the uncertainty around why the move became so extreme.
7. End with the divide between trading “a barrel” on a screen and arranging for an actual barrel to arrive.

## Corporate finance and business economics

### A Profitable Company Can Still Go Bankrupt

- **Pillar:** Familiar concept, missing mechanism
- **Status:** spark — highest-scoring launch candidate
- **Central question:** How can a company report a profit while running out of money?
- **Provisional thesis:** Profit measures economic performance under accounting rules, while bankruptcy arrives when cash obligations cannot be met on time; working capital, growth, debt maturities, and refinancing connect the two.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A profitable company necessarily generates enough cash to survive.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Profitability and liquidity answer different questions, and rapid growth can make the mismatch worse.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The contradiction is immediately legible and broadly useful beyond finance specialists.
- **Likely runtime:** 9–11 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** A simple income statement, cash-flow bridge, working-capital cycle, and a public corporate failure with positive accounting earnings before distress.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Persistent genuine profitability usually creates financing options; the script must distinguish temporary cash timing from a fundamentally unviable business.
- **Visual anchor:** A profitable income statement sits beside an empty bank account and an invoice due today.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F5 = **30/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a company reporting profit while being unable to make a payment.
2. Separate revenue, accounting profit, and cash flow.
3. Explain receivables, inventory, payables, and the working-capital timing gap.
4. Show how growth can consume cash faster than the business collects it.
5. Add capital expenditure, debt maturities, and refinancing risk.
6. Distinguish a temporary liquidity crisis from long-run insolvency.
7. End with the practical divide: profit says the model may work; cash decides whether the company reaches the next reporting date.

### Why Companies Buy Their Own Shares

- **Pillar:** Corporate finance explained through incentives
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** When does a share buyback create value, and when is it expensive financial engineering?
- **Provisional thesis:** A buyback returns capital by reducing shares outstanding; its value depends on the price paid, financing, alternatives forgone, and management incentives.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Buybacks automatically enrich shareholders or automatically manipulate the share price.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A buyback is a capital-allocation decision whose quality cannot be judged without valuation and opportunity cost.
- **Why somebody clicks:** Buybacks are politically controversial, widely misunderstood, and mechanically easy to demonstrate.
- **Likely runtime:** 9–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** A 100-share numerical example, dividend comparison, repurchase disclosures, and examples of buying below versus above intrinsic value.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Even mechanically accretive buybacks may entrench poor management incentives or transfer value between selling and remaining shareholders.
- **Visual anchor:** One hundred ownership slices become ninety, enlarging each remaining slice.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C4 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F5 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open with a company becoming a major buyer of its own shares.
2. Show what happens when 100 shares become 90.
3. Compare the cash flow and flexibility of buybacks with dividends.
4. Explain why buying undervalued shares can create value for remaining holders.
5. Show how repurchasing overvalued shares destroys value despite boosting per-share metrics.
6. Examine executive compensation, debt-funded repurchases, and foregone investment.
7. End with the only useful question: was this the best available use of the cash?

## Everyday economics

### The Same Plane Seat Has 20 Different Prices

- **Pillar:** Economics hidden in ordinary life
- **Status:** spark — early variety
- **Central question:** Why can two passengers in adjacent seats pay radically different prices for the same journey?
- **Provisional thesis:** Airlines use fare classes, restrictions, and changing inventory to separate customers by willingness to pay while managing seats that become worthless at departure.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Airline pricing is arbitrary or based mainly on the cost of carrying each passenger.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** The seat has a near-zero marginal cost once scheduled but a hard expiry, so the airline manages access to different prices rather than assigning one stable price.
- **Why somebody clicks:** Almost everyone has experienced the mystery personally.
- **Likely runtime:** 9–11 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Fare-class documentation, revenue-management reports, advance-purchase restrictions, dynamic availability, and overbooking data.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Revenue management is not purely efficient; market concentration, opacity, ancillary fees, and consumer lock-in affect both prices and fairness.
- **Visual anchor:** One aircraft row with a different price tag above every passenger.
- **Freshness risk:** Medium because industry systems and consumer rules change.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P4 / T5 / E4 / V5 / F5 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Place two adjacent passengers with radically different ticket prices.
2. Establish the perishable-inventory problem: an empty departing seat can never be sold later.
3. Explain fare buckets and changing seat availability.
4. Show the restrictions used to separate flexible leisure travellers from urgent business travellers.
5. Add dynamic demand forecasts and overbooking.
6. Address competition, opacity, fairness, and why price discrimination can also make some cheap tickets possible.
7. End with the idea that travellers are revealing urgency as much as purchasing distance.

### Insurance Needs People Who Don’t Need It

- **Pillar:** Economics hidden in ordinary life
- **Status:** spark
- **Central question:** Why does an insurance pool become unstable when only the people most likely to claim want to join it?
- **Provisional thesis:** Insurance converts uncertain individual losses into predictable pooled costs, but private information can create adverse selection that pushes premiums upward and lower-risk customers outward.
- **Viewer begins believing:** Insurance should naturally attract the people most likely to need it.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A functioning pool also needs people who are unlikely to claim, which creates difficult trade-offs between accurate pricing, access, and compulsory participation.
- **Why somebody clicks:** The title sounds cruel until the pooling mechanism makes it mathematically clear.
- **Likely runtime:** 10–12 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** A simple mixed-risk pool, adverse-selection spiral, deductibles, underwriting, waiting periods, and compulsory coverage structures.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Real insurance markets contain subsidies, regulation, behavioural errors, employer selection, and public objectives that complicate the textbook spiral.
- **Gaming reference, only if useful:** A raid group cannot pool rare-loss costs if only players who already know they will die buy protection.
- **Visual anchor:** Healthy and high-risk figures enter and leave a pool as the premium counter changes.
- **Freshness risk:** Low for the mechanism; legal examples are jurisdiction-sensitive.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P5 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F4 = **28/30**

#### Short outline

1. Begin with an insurer unable to observe who is likely to claim.
2. Explain risk pooling and why many small premiums fund a few large losses.
3. Give customers an informational advantage and build adverse selection numerically.
4. Show the premium spiral as lower-risk customers leave.
5. Explain deductibles, exclusions, underwriting, waiting periods, and risk-based prices.
6. Examine compulsory or employer-based pools as attempts to preserve participation.
7. End on the real policy tension: accurate pricing can exclude the people insurance is meant to protect.

## Financial history

### The Hedge Fund That Was Too Smart to Fail

- **Pillar:** Historical event, reusable model
- **Status:** spark — research-intensive documentary
- **Central question:** How did sophisticated convergence trades become a threat to market stability?
- **Provisional thesis:** LTCM used leverage to amplify small expected convergence gains, but a liquidity shock widened positions, triggered collateral pressure, and made it impossible to wait for the thesis to recover.
- **Viewer begins believing:** The fund failed because its mathematical models simply predicted prices incorrectly.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A trade can be directionally correct in the long run and still fail through leverage, liquidity, crowded positioning, and forced liquidation.
- **Why somebody clicks:** Exceptional intelligence, enormous leverage, and near-collapse create a naturally dramatic story.
- **Likely runtime:** 13–15 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** Federal Reserve testimony, official working-group reports, disclosed leverage and exposures, Russian default context, and the private-sector recapitalization.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Avoid turning LTCM into a simple morality tale about arrogant mathematicians; the size, mechanics, counterparty behaviour, and counterfactual systemic risk remain debated.
- **Visual anchor:** Two prices slowly converge, then violently diverge as a leverage multiplier expands.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P4 / T5 / E5 / V4 / F3 = **26/30**

#### Short outline

1. Introduce the prestigious team and its apparently controlled strategy.
2. Explain convergence trading with one simple pair of instruments.
3. Show why tiny expected gains invite enormous leverage.
4. Introduce the Russian crisis and the flight toward liquidity and safety.
5. Explain widening spreads, collateral calls, and why LTCM could not wait.
6. Show how forced liquidation threatened to move prices further against similar positions.
7. Examine the private rescue and end with the difference between being eventually right and remaining solvent.

### Britain Tried to Fix the Pound. The Market Said No.

- **Pillar:** Historical event, reusable model
- **Status:** spark — historical documentary
- **Central question:** Why could Britain not maintain sterling inside the European Exchange Rate Mechanism in September 1992?
- **Provisional thesis:** The interest-rate and intervention policy required to defend sterling conflicted with British economic conditions, making the exchange-rate commitment increasingly expensive and ultimately politically incredible.
- **Viewer begins believing:** George Soros or speculators overpowered an otherwise sustainable government policy.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** Speculators tested and accelerated a contradiction between the promised exchange rate and the domestic cost of defending it.
- **Why somebody clicks:** It offers a clean contest between state commitment, market incentives, and an explicit breaking point.
- **Likely runtime:** 12–15 minutes
- **Best evidence or case:** ERM rules, British and German interest rates, official intervention, policy announcements on 16 September 1992, and post-event official accounts.
- **Strongest counterargument:** The story must not imply markets always defeat governments; credible pegs can survive when reserves, policy, institutions, and domestic conditions align.
- **Visual anchor:** Sterling repeatedly hits the lower ERM band while rates and reserve spending climb.
- **Freshness risk:** Low.
- **Potential score:** C5 / P4 / T5 / E5 / V5 / F3 = **27/30**

#### Short outline

1. Open during the final hours of the defence of sterling.
2. Explain the ERM as a permitted exchange-rate band.
3. Show the conflict between British recession, German rates, and the chosen sterling parity.
4. Explain how traders test a peg they believe is unsustainable.
5. Present intervention and higher interest rates as the government’s two main defences.
6. Show why the domestic and financial cost undermined credibility.
7. End with withdrawal from the ERM and the lesson that a promised price needs a sustainable policy behind it.
