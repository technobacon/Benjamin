# Franklin — Draft Script: A Profitable Company Can Still Go Bankrupt

## Metadata

- **Working title:** A Profitable Company Can Still Go Bankrupt
- **Thumbnail premise:** `PROFIT: +€150K` beside `€50K SHORT`
- **Central question:** How can a company report a profit while being unable to make its next payment?
- **Thesis:** Profit is measured across a reporting period; survival depends on cash and committed financing being available on particular payment dates.
- **Viewer begins believing:** A profitable company must be generating enough money to survive.
- **Viewer leaves believing:** A company can earn legitimate accounting profit while cash is trapped in its operating cycle, and the timing problem becomes fatal only when it can no longer finance the wait.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Normal timing gaps are routine and usually financeable; persistent genuine profitability tends to create funding options.
- **Target runtime:** approximately 10 minutes
- **Target narration:** approximately 1,500 words
- **Information cutoff:** 12 August 2026
- **Research dossier:** `research/2026-08-12-profitable-company-bankruptcy.md`

## Script

### 0:00–0:30 — Two correct numbers

`[VISUAL: FICTIONAL FINANCIALS — a clean income statement lands on “PROFIT: €150,000”; cut to a supplier payment screen showing “€50,000 SHORT”; Franklin checks both pages]`

At the end of its second month, this company has sold €450,000 worth of chairs. The chairs cost €300,000 to make. So it has earned €150,000 in profit.

It also has a €200,000 supplier payment due today—and €150,000 in the bank.

The company is profitable. The payment will still fail.

And the strange part is that month two was its best month. Sales doubled. That is what created the cash crisis.

### 0:30–1:12 — The payment date

`[VISUAL: CALENDAR AND STATEMENTS — a quarterly income statement stretches across three months; one red supplier due date appears on Day 31]`

That sounds like either bad accounting or a very selective definition of profit. You do not need either explanation.

The income statement collects revenue and expenses across a period: a month, a quarter, a year. Bills are less patient. Each one has a date attached.

So a company can have customers, decent margins and assets worth more than its debts, then discover that none of those assets can make payroll on Friday.

To see how, we need to follow one sale from the factory floor to the bank account. Those two events are often weeks apart.

### 1:12–2:20 — Why the sale appears before the cash

`[VISUAL: TRANSACTION TIMELINE — chair delivered on Day 1; invoice appears; “RECEIVABLE €150” enters the balance sheet; cash arrival sits on Day 61]`

Suppose a chair is delivered today and the customer has 60 days to pay. The company has completed the sale and earned a right to collect the money. Assuming the normal recognition conditions are met, it records revenue today.

The unpaid invoice becomes a receivable: an asset representing cash the customer owes.

The cost of making the chair is recorded in the same period. Revenue minus the related cost gives us profit, even though the bank account has not moved yet.

This is accrual accounting, and it exists for a good reason. If accounts followed cash alone, a company could deliver an entire project in March, collect in May, and appear to do nothing for two months before suddenly becoming extremely productive on payment day.

Accrual accounting records the economic activity when it happens. The cash-flow statement then shows when the money actually moves.

The separation is useful. It also creates a gap that somebody has to finance.

### 2:20–3:55 — Franklin's Chair Company

`[VISUAL: ANIMATED LEDGER — opening cash, units, revenue, cost, profit, receivables and supplier payment shown in fixed rows; only changed numbers move]`

Start Franklin's Chair Company with €250,000 in cash.

On Day 1, it delivers 1,000 chairs at €150 each. Revenue is €150,000. The chairs cost €100 each to produce, so the company records €100,000 of cost and €50,000 of profit.

Its supplier wants payment immediately. That takes €100,000 out of the bank, leaving €150,000. The customer owes the full €150,000, but will not pay until Day 61.

So far, fine. The company has enough cash.

Then demand doubles.

On Day 31, it delivers 2,000 more chairs. That produces €300,000 of revenue, €200,000 of cost and another €100,000 of profit.

Cumulative profit is now €150,000. Receivables have grown to €450,000. The business is selling more, and every chair has a healthy margin.

Then the second supplier invoice arrives: €200,000. The bank account still contains €150,000 because the first customer payment is thirty days away.

It is €50,000 short.

Nothing in this example relies on a fake sale or a customer who refuses to pay. We have simply made the suppliers faster than the customers.

### 3:55–4:58 — When growth consumes cash

`[VISUAL: OPERATING CYCLE — cash becomes inventory, inventory becomes a sale, sale becomes a receivable, receivable returns to cash; the cycle stretches as order volume doubles]`

This gap sits inside working capital: the short-term assets and liabilities created by normal operations.

Receivables consume cash relative to profit because the sale has been recorded but not collected. Inventory consumes cash because it has been bought or produced but not sold. Supplier payables help in the opposite direction by letting the company keep its cash for longer.

That is why growth can be uncomfortable. If each new order needs materials and wages before the customer pays, more sales create a larger financing requirement.

Growth is still valuable. It simply has a price *before* it produces cash. A supermarket can often collect from shoppers before paying suppliers. A manufacturer offering customers long payment terms may need months of funding for the same amount of profit.

The margin tells us whether each sale is attractive. The operating cycle tells us how much cash the company must commit to keep making them.

### 4:58–6:04 — Cash can leave without appearing as today's expense

`[VISUAL: CASH LEDGER — a €1m machine payment leaves in one block; beside it, ten smaller annual depreciation blocks appear; then a debt maturity lands on the calendar]`

Working capital explains the chair company, but an income statement can miss the timing of other large payments too.

Imagine the company buys a €1 million machine and expects to use it for ten years. The cash may leave today. The cost on the income statement is generally spread over the machine's useful life through depreciation. A simplified version might show €100,000 of expense each year while the bank account absorbed the full €1 million at the start.

Debt creates another deadline. Interest is an expense. Repaying the principal is not; it settles a liability on the balance sheet. A company can report a respectable operating profit and still face a bond or loan maturity far larger than the cash it has available.

This is also why EBITDA needs adult supervision. It removes interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation before we even ask about capital expenditure or working capital. Useful in the right context. Less useful when the immediate question is whether money exists.

Dividends and buybacks reduce cash without reducing profit as well. Reading the payment calendar requires several statements. The headline profit number cannot do that job alone.

### 6:04–6:58 — The bridge

`[VISUAL: FUNDING GAP — the €50k hole is crossed by labelled blocks: cash reserve, credit line, supplier extension; the blocks withdraw when a covenant warning appears]`

Our chair company has not reached an inevitable collapse. Its problem is ordinary. A bank can lend the missing €50,000 for thirty days. The supplier can extend its terms. The receivables can sometimes be sold or pledged. Owners can add capital.

Profitable businesses usually have more options because lenders can see a plausible source of repayment.

The situation becomes dangerous when the gap is larger than the cash and financing still available. Perhaps the credit line is already full. Perhaps borrowing more would breach a covenant. A major customer may dispute an invoice, making the receivable worth less to a lender. Or a large debt matures during a market panic and nobody wants to refinance it.

The legal language changes by country. British companies may enter administration or liquidation; American companies may use Chapter 11 to reorganise. The shared financial problem is simpler: an obligation is due, and the company cannot fund it.

### 6:58–8:35 — Carillion: when cash also questions the profit

`[VISUAL: OFFICIAL DOCUMENTS — House of Commons Carillion report; highlight “5–6%”, then borrowing £242m → £689m; keep paragraph numbers and source visible]`

Carillion shows the messier version of this story.

Until July 2017, its audited accounts presented a profitable construction and services company. Reported margins were around 5 to 6 percent. Meanwhile, borrowing had risen from £242 million in December 2009 to £689 million by December 2016.

Its cash conversion looked reassuring too. From 2013 to 2016, Carillion reported £509 million of operating cash inflow against £501 million of group operating profit.

But part of that cash came through an early-payment facility. Banks paid certain suppliers early; Carillion owed the banks later. In its cash-flow statement, the company presented the resulting inflows within operating activities rather than financing activities.

A parliamentary report cited Moody's analysis that reclassifying £472 million of those inflows as financing would leave only £37 million of the £501 million operating profit cash-backed—a conversion rate of 7 percent.

The exact accounting classification depended on the facility terms, and the Financial Reporting Council did not confirm the rating agencies' view. The economic concern was still hard to ignore: fresh finance was doing much of the work attributed to operating cash generation.

On 9 June 2017, Carillion paid a £55 million dividend. One month later, it announced an £845 million write-down on major contracts. Another £200 million followed, wiping out the previous seven years of reported profit.

By January 2018, attempts to secure short-term support had failed. Carillion entered compulsory liquidation with reportedly £29 million left in cash.

Carillion is a different problem from the clean chair-company example. Its historical profit was itself under serious question. That is why the cash-flow gap mattered: sometimes cash is late; sometimes it is warning that the profit may never arrive.

### 8:35–9:28 — What to look for

`[VISUAL: ANNUAL REPORT — four tabs appear: cash flow, working capital, capex, debt; figures are highlighted as each is mentioned]`

When a company reports strong profit, start by comparing it with operating cash flow across several periods. One bad quarter proves very little. A persistent gap needs an explanation.

Then look at what grew. Receivables rising faster than sales can mean customers are taking longer to pay. Inventory building faster than revenue can mean cash is sitting in warehouses. A sudden increase in payables may mean the company is preserving cash by making suppliers wait.

Next, subtract capital expenditure from operating cash flow using a clearly stated definition. Then read the debt-maturity table. How much is due in the next year? How much unused credit is actually committed? Are covenants close to their limits?

Every one of these can have an innocent explanation. New factories consume cash. Seasonal inventory builds. Large customers negotiate longer terms. The useful question is specific: what is consuming the cash, when should it reverse, and who funds the company until then?

### 9:28–10:00 — Thirty days

`[VISUAL: RETURN TO CHAIR COMPANY — Day 31 supplier invoice and Day 61 customer payment sit thirty days apart; the €50k funding block either fills the gap or disappears]`

Back at Franklin's Chair Company, the customer payment on Day 61 would solve the problem. The supplier is due on Day 31.

The entire crisis fits inside those thirty days.

The company can borrow, negotiate, or raise capital to cross them. If it cannot, the supplier may stop deliveries, employees may go unpaid, and a creditor may begin formal proceedings. The orders, margins and receivables do not make the deadline move.

The income statement can say the company had a good year. Payroll only asks whether the money is there on Friday.
