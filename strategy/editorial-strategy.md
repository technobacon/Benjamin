# Franklin — Editorial Strategy

## Positioning

Franklin sits between beginner financial education and specialist market commentary. The channel assumes basic investing familiarity, then goes one causal layer deeper than the usual explainer. It should be accessible without flattening disagreement or hiding the assumptions that make a result work.

The differentiator is the combination of:

- mechanism-first explanation;
- credible numerical and historical research;
- dry, restrained humour;
- a recognizable Benjamin Franklin mascot;
- occasional gaming models that illuminate finance without changing the subject.

## Launch objective

Build a library of evergreen videos that establish trust and teach the audience how Franklin thinks. Early videos should answer questions with durable search and recommendation value. Current news can supply an opening example, but the useful life of the video should outlast the headline.

## Priority content pillars

### 1. Familiar concept, missing mechanism

Take a term viewers recognize and reveal the machinery they probably do not.

Examples: how banks create deposits; why bond prices fall when yields rise; what a central-bank balance sheet actually does; why liquidity disappears when everybody needs it.

### 2. Historical event, reusable model

Use one episode to teach a mechanism that appears elsewhere.

Examples: a bank run as a coordination problem; Bretton Woods as a constraint system; LTCM as leverage plus crowded positioning; the 2008 repo market as hidden bank plumbing.

### 3. Everyday price, wider system

Begin with something ordinary and trace the institutions behind it.

Examples: mortgage rates, insurance premiums, surge pricing, subscription discounts, airline tickets, or supermarket shrinkflation.

### 4. Virtual economy, real lesson

Use a game only when its simplified rules make a real mechanism visible.

Examples: inflation from resource faucets, transaction costs in auction houses, speculative bubbles around updates, price discrimination in free-to-play games, or principal-agent problems in guilds.

## Early topics to avoid

- daily market recaps and predictions;
- stock picks or portfolio content;
- party-political reaction videos;
- generic “five money habits” advice;
- career stories built around the creator's employer;
- subjects whose decisive evidence depends on non-public professional knowledge;
- technical subjects selected mainly to prove expertise.

These can be revisited only when the channel has a clear reason and an appropriate research or review process.

## Topic selection scorecard

Score each category from 1 to 5. A strong idea usually reaches 22/30 without relying on trend timing.

| Category | Question |
|---|---|
| Curiosity | Is there a contradiction, mystery, or surprising outcome? |
| Viewer payoff | Will the viewer gain a reusable mental model? |
| Thesis strength | Can the video make one specific, arguable claim? |
| Evidence | Are strong primary sources and useful numbers available? |
| Visual potential | Can the mechanism be shown with charts, documents, archives, or simple diagrams? |
| Production fit | Can a solo creator make the video well within the planned schedule? |

Reject or reshape an idea if evidence scores below 3, even when the title is excellent.

## Packaging rules

### Titles

Prefer a concrete object plus a hidden mechanism, a surprising declarative claim, or a familiar question made specific.

Useful shapes:

- “Why [familiar thing] behaves strangely”
- “The [ordinary object] that explains [large system]”
- “How [historical decision] broke [constraint]”
- “[Provocative statement stated plainly]”

Avoid textbook labels such as “An Introduction to Monetary Policy” and empty hype such as “The INSANE Truth About Banks.”

### Thumbnails

- One idea, readable at phone size.
- Franklin supplies character or reaction; evidence supplies credibility.
- Prefer a number, object, document, or simple visual contradiction.
- Do not repeat the title word for word.
- Do not promise scandal when the video explains a trade-off.

## Recurring formats

- **Franklin Explains** — a familiar concept with its missing mechanism.
- **How This Broke** — a historical system under stress.
- **The Price of…** — incentives and trade-offs hidden inside an ordinary product or policy.
- **Economies With Rules** — occasional virtual-economy episodes linked to real economic models.

The series label should help planning. It does not need to appear in every public title.

## Success criteria

Evaluate more than views:

- click-through rate relative to topic size;
- retention through the first explanation and the main turn;
- comments that accurately restate or challenge the thesis;
- returning viewers across different pillars;
- research corrections required after publication;
- production hours per finished minute;
- whether the video still feels useful six months later.

## Editorial review questions

Before approving a topic:

1. What does the viewer currently misunderstand?
2. What mechanism resolves that misunderstanding?
3. What evidence could disprove the preferred thesis?
4. Why is video the right format for this explanation?
5. Can the title remain honest after every important qualification is included?
